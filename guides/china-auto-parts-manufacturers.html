Industry overview: where China's auto parts are actually made

"China" is not one auto parts market — it's several regional clusters, each with different strengths. Knowing the region a factory sits in tells you a lot before you even see a factory photo.

RegionKnown forNotes for buyers
Guangdong (Guangzhou, Foshan, Shenzhen)Aftermarket parts, LED lighting, body kits, decalsDense export and logistics infrastructure; easy to combine a factory visit with port access
Zhejiang & JiangsuAutomotive components, fasteners, electronics, small partsLarge base of specialized component factories, often supplying multiple assemblers
Chongqing & SichuanWheels, castings, drivetrain parts, motorcycle-derived manufacturingLong industrial history in vehicle parts; inland, so factor in extra transit to port
ShandongTires, heavy-vehicle and truck partsStrong for commercial vehicle and tire sourcing specifically

Registered address and production address don't always match — a company can be registered in one city with a factory in another, or a sales office in a district known more for offices than plants. That's not automatically a problem, but it's exactly the kind of gap a listing page won't show you.

Manufacturer categories

Different part categories need different manufacturing equipment, so a supplier that's strong in one is not necessarily equipped for another — a fact "one-stop-shop" listings can obscure.

Braking

Brake pads & rotors

Requires friction-material mixing, hot-press and curing equipment (pads) or casting/machining lines (rotors). See our brake pad manufacturer guide.

Wheels

Wheels & spare wheel kits

Steel or alloy casting/forging lines. Offset, center bore and PCD must be confirmed in writing — see our spare wheel case study.

Suspension

Suspension & steering parts

Forged or machined components under tight tolerances; material certificates and load testing matter more than on cosmetic parts.

EV / NEV

EV aftermarket & NEV parts

A newer category with less standardized aftermarket coverage; fitment data is often specific to a single model year and trim.

Supplier evaluation checklist

This is what we actually check, in roughly this order, before telling a client whether to proceed.

  1. Factory capability — does the supplier run the relevant production equipment in-house, or source the part and repack/resell it? Ask for a live video walkthrough of the specific process, not a generic factory tour clip.
  2. Export experience — has this supplier actually shipped to your destination market before, and do they know the paperwork it requires?
  3. Certifications — for your specific destination market and part category (see our brake pad certification table for an example). A certificate you can't look up by number isn't proof.
  4. MOQ per part number — a headline MOQ often applies to standard catalog parts, not custom specs, packaging or private label. Confirm per SKU, in writing.
  5. Communication & written specs — will the supplier put dimensions, materials and tolerances in writing, and re-check them if you push back? Our BYD Seagull case study shows why this step alone can catch a real error.

FAQ

Where are most Chinese auto parts manufactured?

Guangdong (Guangzhou, Foshan, Shenzhen) is strong in aftermarket parts, lighting and body components. Zhejiang and Jiangsu have dense component clusters. Chongqing and Sichuan have a long vehicle-manufacturing base, useful for wheels, castings and drivetrain parts. Shandong is a major hub for tires and heavy-vehicle parts.

How do I evaluate a Chinese auto parts supplier before ordering?

Check factory capability, export experience to your market, relevant certifications, realistic MOQ per part number, and whether they'll put specifications in writing. A supplier who won't document specs or show production on video is a risk regardless of price.

What's the difference between a manufacturer and a trading company for auto parts?

A manufacturer runs the actual production equipment for the category. A trading company sources from one or more manufacturers and resells, often across a wider range than one factory could produce. Neither is automatically bad — but pricing, lead time and quality control responsibility differ.
