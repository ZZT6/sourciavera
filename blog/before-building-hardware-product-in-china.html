Before Building a Hardware Product in China, Check If It Already Exists

Sep 9, 2026 · 7 min read · Zhang Peng, Founder
Back to Blog

People keep asking whether startups should manufacture hardware in China. It comes up on Quora and anywhere else founders gather, so I'll answer it directly: I think it's usually the wrong question. Most hardware startups don't need to build their own manufacturing in China at all. They need to find out whether a supplier ecosystem for something close to their product already exists, and whether the factory they're about to trust really has the capability they're assuming.

The short answer: before you commit to custom manufacturing, check whether your product category already has a mature supply chain in China. Most consumer hardware does, often down to components you assumed you'd have to design from scratch. If it exists, your job is choosing and verifying a supplier, not building a factory. If it genuinely doesn't exist because your product is a real mechanical or electrical novelty, the cost and timeline conversation changes completely, and you should know that before you start.

Why Is "Can China Make This?" the Wrong Starting Point?

China's manufacturing base covers so much ground that "can they build it" is rarely the real bottleneck. The question I'd rather hear is whether a factory somewhere in Shenzhen, Dongguan, or Yiwu is already tooled up to produce something 80% identical to your idea: a PCBA design, an injection-molded enclosure, a battery pack configuration. Someone else may already have paid to develop it.

Founders who skip this step end up getting quotes for custom tooling and NRE costs in a category where three factories already have the mold on a shelf and would happily put your logo on it instead.

A Typical Case: "Nobody Makes a Product Like Mine"

You see this most clearly in categories with mature, commoditized supply chains: charging cables and power banks, Bluetooth speaker modules, LED lighting drivers, injection-molded enclosures in standard sizes, PCBA reference designs for common sensor combinations.

  • The problem: founders come to us completely certain they need a custom production line for, say, a pet GPS tracker, convinced nobody else makes anything close.
  • What we checked: a few searches by component, not by finished product. In that pet-tracker case, there were four factories in Shenzhen already running GPS tracker PCBA lines at volume.
  • The outcome: all they needed from the founder was firmware, a case design, and a logo. No new production line.
  • The takeaway: the manufacturing risk was never "can this be built." It was which of those four factories was actually good, and whether the one with the sharpest Alibaba photos would still answer messages after the third production run. In my experience, it usually isn't the same one.

When Does a Hardware Startup Really Need Custom Manufacturing?

Some products really are new enough that no existing supply chain covers them: a new mechanical actuation method, a material combination nobody has tooled for, a form factor that doesn't fit any standard mold. If that's you, the math changes:

  • Expect to pay for tooling and NRE (non-recurring engineering) costs.
  • Expect a longer wait before your first sellable unit.
  • Expect to need a much closer relationship with the factory, because you're asking them to build capability just for you rather than adapt something they already run.

In my experience, only a small minority of the founders who come to us convinced they're in this group actually are. Most have a pet tracker, a smart water bottle, or a fitness band. Those supply chains have been around so long that half of Shenzhen could quote them from memory.

Why Do Hardware Startups Fail When Sourcing in China?

The startups that struggle with sourcing in China rarely fail because they couldn't find a factory. They fail because they found a factory, with good sample photos and a fast WhatsApp reply, and never confirmed it was the right one before betting a production run on it.

A sample built by a factory's best technician on their best day, in a small batch, tells you very little about what a 5,000-unit run looks like three months later under a different production schedule. That's especially true once you're no longer their only customer, or their newest one.

It's the same gap I cover in more detail in my 10 things to check before paying a supplier deposit. Company legitimacy, factory-versus-trading-company status, and production capacity matter just as much for a hardware startup's first PCBA run as they do for a container of auto parts.

What Sequence Should a Hardware Founder Follow?

  • Search first, tool second. Before you ask for a single quote, spend time on 1688 and Alibaba searching for your product in pieces (the enclosure, the PCBA, the battery, the connector) rather than as the whole assembled idea. That's usually how the supply chain is actually organized.
  • Shortlist three to five candidate factories per component, not one.
  • Ask each one directly whether they already produce something similar for another brand. Treat hesitation on that question the same way you'd treat hesitation when you ask for a business license.
  • Run a small pilot order before you commit to full production.
  • Verify the factory in person, or through someone who can, before that pilot order. Not after it ships with a problem already built in.

What I've Learned From Watching Founders Do This

As the founder, I spend a lot of time on factory floors myself, alongside our team in Guangzhou. The founders I've seen skip the existence question aren't lazy. They're moving fast, which is the right instinct almost everywhere except here.

The ones who search first tend to end up sourcing from Shenzhen or Dongguan instead of paying for a mold in some third-tier city just because that's where the first quote came from. Same product, sometimes even the same price. But one supplier has made that exact part 40,000 times, and the other is learning your tolerances as they go, on your production run.

If I could give a hardware founder one habit, it would be this: spend a week proving your product doesn't already exist before you spend months building it.
