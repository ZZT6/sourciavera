If you are about to send a first deposit, it is normal to feel unsure about what you should have checked by now. Most buyers are never taught this, and a supplier who is pleasant to deal with can make asking for proof feel rude. It isn't: a supplier with nothing to hide expects these questions. You do not need all 46 checks on every order, so start with the stages that match the size of your order and stop when the answers stop adding up.

How Is the Checklist Organized, and How Should You Use It?

The six stages follow the order in which a deposit decision actually happens, from "who is this company?" to "does what ships match what I approved?". Tick a box only when you have seen the evidence yourself or through an independent third party, and write a note next to anything you could not confirm. No single check is proof of anything, so look at how several of them line up.

Stage 1: Company Identity

Why check it: a deposit goes to a legal entity, not to a sales account on a platform. If the names on the license, the invoice and the bank account are not the same company, you cannot be sure who you are contracting with.

The most common oversight: treating a badge or an online profile as the check. Alibaba says its Verified Supplier audits are done by third-party inspectors and that it does not guarantee the accuracy of what they report, and in one check our team ran, four Verified Supplier storefronts that presented themselves as manufacturers were registered as trading or import-export entities. The license and the registry record are where that shows up.

The registry lookup in practice: China's National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (gsxt.gov.cn) is free to search, but it is in Chinese only and can be hard to open from outside mainland China. Ask the supplier for the full Chinese company name exactly as printed on the license, and if the site will not load for you, ask a Chinese-speaking colleague or an agent to run the lookup. The documents themselves are covered in 8 Documents You Should Always Request From a Chinese Manufacturer.

Stage 2: Factory or Trading Company

Why check it: a factory, a trading company and a reseller can quote different prices, control different parts of production and answer for quality in different ways. A trading company can be a fine partner. The problem starts when you think you are buying from a factory and you are not.

The most common oversight: asking "are you a factory?" and accepting "yes". The answer proves nothing, so the checklist asks for things that are harder to fake: which factory makes your product and where, a specific technical answer, a live look at the line, and a registered address that fits a factory. The signs are covered in Is My Chinese Supplier a Real Factory or a Trading Company? and 6 Signs Your "Factory" Is Actually a Trading Company in Disguise, and why the difference matters in Trading Company vs Factory: Why It Matters More Than You Think.

Stage 3: Production Capability

Why check it: a real factory can still be too small, too busy or too different from its photos to make your order on time and to specification. Capacity and subcontracting are where that shows up.

The most common oversight: accepting photos, a recorded tour or a visit that was arranged for you. A live video call on a day you choose, with a handwritten note held up on camera, shows what a prepared presentation does not. What a call can and cannot reveal is covered in 5 Things a Live Video Call Can Reveal That Factory Photos Can't, and how factories handle visiting buyers in Inside a Chinese Factory's Visitor Playbook — And the Line That Gives Away a Trading Company.

Stage 4: Samples and Specifications

Why check it: a sample proves that the factory can make your product once, by hand, when it knows you are watching. It says very little about what 1,000 units off the line will look like.

The most common oversight: approving a sample without a written specification and a sealed reference sample, so nothing exists to hold the bulk order against. Write the specification into the order confirmation, ask for a production sample from the real run, and agree in writing what happens if the bulk shipment does not match. The full method is in Sample Looks Great, Bulk Order Is Garbage — How to Prevent It.

Stage 5: Contract and Payment

Why check it: this is where the money leaves your account, and what you have agreed in writing is what you can point to later.

The most common oversight: paying into an account whose name does not match the company, or relying on chat messages instead of a formal order. If you buy through Alibaba.com, Alibaba says Trade Assurance applies when you pay through its secure payment platform, so a request to pay outside the platform (straight into a bank account, by Western Union or in cryptocurrency) takes you outside that protection. The warning signs are in 9 Alibaba Supplier Red Flags That Should Make You Walk Away, the ten checks before a deposit are in 10 Things to Check Before Paying a Chinese Supplier Deposit, and what Trade Assurance does and does not cover is in Is Alibaba Trustworthy? An Honest Answer for First-Time Buyers.

Stage 6: Before Shipment

Why check it: before shipment you still hold the balance. Once the goods have left, your options shrink.

The most common oversight: paying the balance before anyone independent has looked at the goods, or letting the factory's own quality-control team be the only inspector. Agree the inspection standard in writing before production starts. The inspection types and what each covers are in What Does Factory Inspection in China Cost? (And Is It Worth It), and a worked example of an AQL sampling check is in 6 Quality Checks Every Auto Parts Buyer Should Run Before Shipping.

What Can't a Checklist Tell You?

A checklist tells you what to ask for, not whether the answer is true. A license can be a copy of another company's, a video can show another factory's floor, and a certificate can cover a different model. That is why the checklist keeps asking for evidence you can cross-check: the same company name on the license, the invoice and the bank account, and the same address in the registry, on the map and on the video call. When an order is large, or the product has to meet a tight specification, the checks that count most are the ones where an independent person looks with their own eyes, which is what our due diligence checklist and our supplier verification guide explain.

What Should You Do Next?

  1. Download the checklist and print one copy per supplier you are comparing, so the answers sit side by side.
  2. Start with stages 1 and 2. They cost only time and often remove a supplier before any money is at stake.
  3. Book the live video call (stage 3) only with the suppliers that pass, on a day you choose.
  4. Put the specification, the sample conditions and the payment terms in writing (stages 4 and 5) before you pay anything.
  5. Plan the inspection (stage 6) before production starts, and keep the balance until you have accepted the report.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I check before paying a deposit to a Chinese supplier?

Check six things in order: who the company legally is, whether it makes the product or resells it, whether it can produce your order, whether the sample and the specification are written down, whether the contract and the payment account match the company, and how the goods will be inspected before you pay the balance. The free checklist on this page turns those into 46 items with tick boxes.

How do I verify a Chinese supplier?

Start with the business license and look the company up in China's National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System. Then confirm on a live video call, on a day you choose, that the factory exists and makes your product, and have an independent inspector check the goods before you pay the balance. No single check is proof, so look at how several of them line up.

How do I check a Chinese business license?

Ask the supplier for a clear copy of the license, which carries the full Chinese company name and an 18-character Unified Social Credit Code. Search the name or the code in the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (gsxt.gov.cn) and compare the record with the copy: name, address, registered capital, date of establishment and business scope. The registry is in Chinese only and can be hard to open from outside mainland China, so you may need a Chinese-speaking colleague or an agent to run the lookup.

How do I verify a supplier on Alibaba?

Treat the badge as a starting point, not as the check. Alibaba says its Verified Supplier audits are done by third-party inspectors and that it does not guarantee the accuracy of what they report. Run the same checks as for any supplier, and if you pay, pay through Alibaba.com's secure payment platform so that Trade Assurance can apply.

Can I run these checks without visiting China?

Many of them, yes. The license, the registry lookup, the map check, the written specification, the payment terms and the documents can all be done from your desk, and a live video call covers part of the production checks. The checks that need someone physically present are the factory visit and the inspection of the goods, which an independent inspector or a local representative can do for you.
