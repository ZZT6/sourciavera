CBP Wants Low-Value Imports Filed at Arrival, With a Bond

By Zhang Peng (Miguel Lopez), Founder · Oct 23, 2026 · Updated Oct 24, 2026 · 9 min read

The short answer: With de minimis suspended, CBP proposed on October 8, 2026 that imports worth $2,500 or less be filed on or before arrival, with a bond. It is only a proposal: comments close December 7, 2026. If you ship small parcels from China, get the deliver-to details and invoice data right before they land.

Verified as of October 24, 2026 against the proposed rule in the Federal Register (91 FR 64532, published October 8, 2026). What comes from the trade press is marked "according to".

This article covers what the notice proposes, what is still open and what to ask your Chinese supplier and your forwarder to provide now. It is based on the notice itself, one trade-press report and general good practice, not on any first-hand experience of these filings. Your licensed customs broker decides how it applies to you.

Why Does It Matter to a U.S. Buyer?

  • Less time to get the paperwork right. Today a Type 11 entry can be filed within 15 days after arrival; the proposal replaces that with "upon or prior to the date of importation" (Federal Register notice).
  • A bond becomes part of your cost. The notice says all Type 11 and Type 13 filers would need a basic importation and entry bond, single-transaction or continuous, and sets minimum liquidated damages of $1,000 for each breach of a listed bond condition. It does not say what a bond will cost you.
  • Delays are a real risk. The Loadstar reports that CBP's own sampling found more than half of the Type 11 imports sampled between September and November 2025 would have missed the new deadline, and that CBP acknowledged some importers may not receive their shipments (The Loadstar).

What Did CBP Propose?

The notice, published at 91 FR 64532 (Docket USCBP-2026-0298), proposes these changes, as we read it (Federal Register; we read the full text on the Federal Register site on October 24, 2026):

  • Type 11 filed at arrival. The 15-day post-arrival window would be replaced by a deadline of "upon or prior to the date of importation", filed electronically. Informal entry stays tied to goods valued at $2,500 or less.
  • A new Type 13 for mail. A new "Informal Mail Entry" for mail valued at $2,500 or less that would otherwise qualify for Type 11. The owner or purchaser may self-file or use a licensed broker, the sender's identity and address and the UPU S-10 tracking number would be required, and mail without a timely, proper entry would be treated as voluntarily abandoned after 15 days.
  • The final deliver-to party. The filer would have to name the final deliver-to party, with its address, when it differs from the ultimate consignee.
  • A bond. A basic importation and entry bond for every Type 11 and Type 13 filer.

Which Dates Are in Play?

  • December 7, 2026: the comment deadline for the proposal.
  • September 22, 2026: start of CBP's voluntary Entry Type 13 test for mail, according to the notice (91 FR 38007); the proposed Type 13 process is built on it. The notice gives no effective date for the new Type 11 and Type 13 requirements.
  • July 1, 2027: the date on which, under the law the notice cites, the de minimis exemption ends; the notice says the exemption has been suspended since at least August 29, 2025 and was suspended indefinitely by CBP rules on June 24, 2026.

What Is Still Unknown?

The final text and its effective date, what a bond will cost and who will offer it, how forwarders and brokers will collect the new data, and whether the rule will change before it is final. The Loadstar quotes a freight company as saying the era of lightweight clearance for parcels has closed and advising shippers to check their systems and their exposure to bonding; that is one company's view. Plan with your broker, not with a date from a news report.

What Should a Buyer Ask the Supplier and the Forwarder For?

This section is general advice, not a rule from the notice. Most of what the proposal asks for starts with information your supplier holds.

  • The final deliver-to party's full name and address, correct and the same on every document. If you ship to a warehouse or a customer, say so before the order is packed.
  • An invoice that shows what you paid. The commercial invoice, the proforma invoice and your payment should agree (see how to pay a Chinese supplier).
  • The sender's name and address as it will appear on the label, and a tracking number for every parcel, sent as soon as it is known.
  • A clear product description and the supplier's tariff code, so your broker can classify the goods without asking twice (the documents to request from a Chinese manufacturer is a good checklist).
  • Shipping data before departure, not after. Ask the forwarder when the entry data is needed and who will transmit it.
  • Who files and who holds the bond. Ask your broker or forwarder whether they will file for you and how the bond works for your shipments.

What Does the Proposal Not Tell You?

It does not tell you what duty you will pay on a given product, how much a bond costs or when the rule will apply. It does not say that your shipments will be held, only that the time to file is shorter. And it says nothing about your supplier.

What Should You Do Next?

  1. Ask your broker or forwarder how they will handle Type 11 filing at arrival and the bond for your shipments.
  2. List your open orders that ship as parcels and check that each has a full deliver-to name and address.
  3. Line up invoice, PI and payment with your supplier, with the price term written down (see which price term to ask a Chinese supplier to quote).
  4. Ask suppliers for tracking numbers and sender details as soon as parcels are packed.
  5. Check any new supplier before you pay, and consider commenting on the proposal through your trade association if it affects your business. Freight is moving too: see China-US ocean and air rates in late October. For refunds of IEEPA tariffs already paid, see how importers claim them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What did CBP propose for low-value imports?

On October 8, 2026 CBP published a proposed rule: Type 11 informal entries for goods worth $2,500 or less would be filed electronically on or before the date of importation, all Type 11 and new Type 13 (mail) filers would need a basic importation and entry bond, and the final deliver-to party would be named. Comments are open until December 7, 2026.

Is this rule final?

No. It is a notice of proposed rulemaking, with comments open until December 7, 2026, and it gives no effective date for the new Type 11 and Type 13 requirements. CBP separately began a voluntary Entry Type 13 test for mail on September 22, 2026.

When does the de minimis exemption end?

The notice says it ends on July 1, 2027 under the law it cites, and that the exemption has been suspended since at least August 29, 2025.

How much will the bond cost?

The notice requires a basic importation and entry bond, single-transaction or continuous, but the sources we read do not say what it will cost. Ask your broker or a surety.

What should I ask my Chinese supplier to provide?

The final deliver-to party's name and address, an invoice that matches your payment, the sender details and a tracking number for every parcel, and a clear product description with the tariff code. Ask your forwarder when the data is needed.

Summary and Next Step

CBP has proposed that low-value Type 11 entries be filed on or before arrival, with a bond and the final deliver-to party named, and a new Type 13 for mail. It is not final, and the end of the de minimis exemption is set for July 1, 2027. For a buyer from China the preparation is practical: correct deliver-to details, an invoice that matches your payment, tracking numbers and a supplier you have checked.
