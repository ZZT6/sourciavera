9 Alibaba Supplier Red Flags That Should Make You Walk Away

Jun 16, 2026 · 8 min read · Zhang Peng, Founder
Back to Blog

Most buyers look for red flags as if they were hunting for a scammer. I think that's the wrong frame. In my experience, the more common problem isn't an outright fraud — it's a real company that is simply wrong for your order: wrong scale, wrong equipment, wrong quality tier, or not a factory at all. The same warning signs catch both.

So I don't treat this list as a fraud detector. I treat it as a filter. The nine flags below follow the order you'd actually hit them: first when screening a profile, then in early conversation, then when you get to photos, paperwork and payment terms.

The short answer: the biggest Alibaba supplier red flags are a new profile with no order history, a business license that says trading instead of manufacturing, refusal of live video, stock factory photos, and pressure to pay fast. No single flag proves fraud, but any two on the same supplier mean you should verify the factory before a deposit moves.

None of these nine, on its own, proves fraud. Any two of them together on the same supplier is where due diligence stops being optional.

How Do Red Flags Stack Up in a Real Case?

The problem: a client came to our team with four Alibaba suppliers for 50% hydrogen peroxide. All four had the "Verified Supplier" badge, similar pricing, similar photos, and each claimed in-house manufacturing. On the platform, nothing separated them. The client had been preparing a 30% deposit to one of them.

What we checked: each business license against China's National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System, and live production video from each supplier.

The outcome: none of the four had a manufacturing scope covering hydrogen peroxide — all four were registered as trading or import-export entities. When we asked for live production video, three couldn't provide it; the fourth sent an old promotional video that turned out to belong to a different company. All four storefronts were reselling from the same handful of real manufacturers.

The takeaway: that's flag #3 (a registration that says trading, not manufacturing) and flag #6 (a factory video that belonged to someone else) on the same supplier — invisible from the storefront, obvious once someone looked. Here's why each flag earns its place.

What Should You Check on a Supplier's Alibaba Profile?

Red Flag #1 — The Profile Was Created Recently

New storefront, aggressive pricing, no order history. Real manufacturers build Alibaba profiles over years — transaction history, response-time records, accumulated buyer reviews. A profile created in the last 6 months with no verified orders should be treated as completely unproven, whatever its self-written description says.

Red Flag #2 — They Claim to Manufacture Everything

A factory in Guangzhou that makes electronics, furniture, textiles and automotive parts doesn't exist. Specialization is the rule in Chinese manufacturing — clusters of factories in specific regions focus on specific product categories. If the catalog spans 12 unrelated categories, you're dealing with a trading company sourcing from multiple factories, and it may not vet its own supply chain any more carefully than you're vetting it.

Red Flag #3 — No Verifiable Business Registration

Every legitimate Chinese manufacturer has a business registration (营业执照) you can request. If they won't share it, or the name doesn't match their Alibaba profile or payment account, stop. China's National Enterprise Credit Information System allows basic public company lookups — a real factory will be there. The registration should show manufacturing in the registered business scope. If it shows trading or consulting, you're not dealing with a factory.

Which Warning Signs Show Up in the First Conversation?

Red Flag #4 — They Can't Answer Basic Technical Questions

Ask something product-specific: "What alloy grade do you use for this bracket?" "What LED chip brand do you source?" "What lumen output tolerance do you hold in production?" A factory that makes your product answers immediately and specifically. A trading company has to "check with the factory" — which tells you exactly where you sit in the supply chain, and how much visibility you'll have if something goes wrong.

Red Flag #5 — Slow Response During China Business Hours

A real factory's sales team handles inquiries during Guangzhou working hours (UTC+8, 9am–6pm Monday–Friday). If replies consistently come only outside those hours, or never within a 4-hour window during business hours, the seller may be a part-time operation, a reseller in another timezone, or someone running multiple storefronts from a laptop. Responsiveness is one of the few things you can verify from a screen.

What Red Flags Appear in Photos, Contracts and Payment Terms?

Red Flag #6 — Factory Photos Are Stock or Appear on Multiple Profiles

A factory proud of its operation sends photos of its actual floor — specific machines, specific workers, specific products in progress. If a reverse image search finds the same photos on other storefronts, competitor profiles or stock photo sites, the "factory" you're looking at isn't the operation you'd be sending money to. Ask for a live walkthrough video at a specific time instead.

Red Flag #7 — MOQ Is Much Lower Than Industry Standard

Minimum order quantities exist because production runs have real setup costs. If the industry standard is 500 units and this supplier will do 10, either they're liquidating overstock or they're not actually manufacturing. Low MOQ can be a genuine feature in some contexts — or a sign that the "factory" is a trader with no production floor.

Red Flag #8 — No Written Contract, Just Chat

A supplier that resists putting order terms in writing on the Alibaba platform is avoiding the documentation trail Trade Assurance needs to process a dispute. Legitimate suppliers have no reason to avoid documentation — it protects them too. Insist on a formal order confirmation with product specifications, quantity, quality standards, packaging requirements and delivery timeline. If they won't do it, walk.

Red Flag #9 — They Create Artificial Urgency Around Payment

"Prices increase next week." "We only have capacity for one more buyer this quarter." "Our manager approved this deal, but only until Friday." Real urgency exists in manufacturing — production slots and material prices do move. But urgency tied to a payment deadline from an unverified supplier is almost always pressure designed to get you past your own due diligence.

What I've Learned

These flags don't always mean fraud. Some mean inexperience; some mean mismatched scale. But from what our team sees on the ground, I've come to trust one rule: when two of them show up on the same supplier, you either walk away or get someone's eyes on the factory before any money moves.

And I've noticed something about the factories themselves. The good ones don't take due diligence as an insult — they expect it, because every experienced importer does it before every significant order. The factories that welcome it are the ones worth working with.
