Factory vs. Trader

Is My Chinese Supplier a Real Factory or a Trading Company?

Jun 10, 2026 · 8 min read · Zhang Peng, Founder
Back to Blog

Most buyers try to find out whether a supplier is a factory by asking, "Are you a factory?" I think that's the least useful question you can ask. Everyone says yes. Trading companies use professional photos taken at real factories, quote prices that look competitive, and describe "our production line" with total confidence. Their margin, typically 15–30%, still comes out of your pocket.

My view: stop asking what they are, and start testing what they can do. A factory can prove things a trader can't. The six clues below are the ones I rely on before I ever get in the car to visit.

The short answer: you can't tell from what a supplier says, only from what it can prove. Check the company name on the business license, the breadth of the catalog, the registered address, and whether they'll show your product's line on a live video call. When three or four of these clues point the same way, they're rarely wrong.

Why Does It Matter Whether Your Supplier Is a Factory?

Factories control production, can customize products, offer lower MOQs on their own lines, and can troubleshoot quality problems directly. Trading companies add a markup, have less control over quality, and often can't offer real OEM options. Knowing which you're dealing with changes your negotiation strategy entirely.

When an order goes wrong (a defect, a delay, a spec mismatch), a factory can act on it immediately. A trading company has to relay the problem through its own relationship with the factory. That adds time, miscommunication risk, and often less urgency, because the financial pain is on your side, not theirs.

What Happens When Six Alibaba Listings Look the Same?

The problem: a buyer shortlisted six Alibaba suppliers for the same product, all with similar listings and comparable prices.

What we checked: our team compared all six side by side on the same clues: license scope, registered address, and production video.

The outcome: two were subcontracting the exact same production line under different storefront names. One quoted 25%+ below the others and turned out to be registered as trading/wholesale, with no manufacturing scope for the product at all. Two others sent a pre-recorded tour that turned out to be the same factory floor filmed from two different accounts.

The takeaway: none of that was visible in the listings. It showed up only when the same clues were checked on every candidate. Here is what those clues look like:

Clue 1 — What Does the Company Name Reveal?

Chinese company names reveal a lot. A name containing 贸易 (màoyì = trade) or 商贸 (shāngmào = commercial trade) is almost certainly a trading company. A name with 制造 (zhìzào = manufacturing), 实业 (shíyè = industry), or 科技 (kējì = technology) plus a factory product category is more likely a manufacturer.

It isn't definitive, but it's a fast first filter. Ask for the business license, read the company name carefully, and verify the full name on China's company registration system at gsxt.samr.gov.cn.

Clue 2 — Does the Catalog Look Like a Department Store?

A real LED headlight factory makes LED headlights, maybe a few closely related products, and that's it. If an Alibaba supplier sells LED lights, car seats, steering wheel covers, and phone holders, it is almost certainly a trader buying from several factories and reselling to you.

Genuine manufacturers have deep but narrow ranges, because they've invested in machines, molds, and know-how for one category. If the catalog looks like a department store, it's a trader's catalog.

Clue 3 — Will They Show Your Production Line Live?

Ask directly: "Can I see the production line for my specific product on a live video call?" A factory says yes and schedules it. A trader says "our factory partner doesn't allow visits" or sends a generic video that doesn't show your product being made.

Then push further: ask to see raw materials arriving, the intermediate assembly stages, and the quality control area. A factory has all of these and can walk you through them. A trader has none of them and will deflect.

Clue 4 — What Do the Price Quotes Tell You?

Traders often quote a price close to, or slightly above, a factory's price, because they need a margin but also need to look competitive. If one of three quotes is notably lower, that supplier might actually be the factory. If one sits oddly in between, it may be a trader undercutting another trader. And, as the container-home case showed, a quote far below everyone else can be a warning sign rather than a bargain.

Ask for a breakdown: raw material, labor, packaging, and factory margin. A factory answers with real numbers. A trader gives vague round numbers, because they only know the wholesale price they pay.

Clue 5 — Does the Registered Address Match a Factory?

The "registered address" on the business license should match a physical factory, not a shared office building in the commercial center of Shenzhen or Shanghai. Look it up on Google Maps Street View: factory compound or office park?

Industrial zones in Guangdong typically have compound walls, loading docks, and row buildings with skylights. Office parks have lobbies, parking lots, and floor directories. The difference is usually visible from satellite view.

Clue 6 — How Fast Can They Handle Customization?

Real factories can adjust molds, packaging, colors, and labeling because they own the machines. Traders have to ask their factory partner, which takes time, introduces errors, and limits your options. If a supplier says "all customization is possible" but goes silent for three days every time you ask for a sample adjustment, they're not in control of production.

Test this early with a small, specific request: a logo on the packaging, a color variant, a dimension change. Time how long a real answer takes. A factory responds within hours. A trader disappears to negotiate with its supplier.

Is a Third-Party Factory Audit Worth It?

Short of visiting in person, the most reliable method is a third-party factory audit: someone goes to the address, confirms the production equipment exists, checks the business license, and confirms the supplier makes what it claims. Some auditors charge $200–$400 per factory visit, and our own standard rate is $499 per man-day: small next to a container of goods worth tens of thousands of dollars ordered from the wrong type of supplier.

What I've learned, on my own visits and from what our team sees on the ground, is that no single clue is proof. A trader can have a factory-sounding name; a factory can be slow to reply. But when three or four clues point the same way, they're rarely wrong. Compare suppliers side by side, test instead of asking, and let the evidence decide.
