How to Verify an Alibaba Supplier Before You Pay (2026 Guide)

Jun 16, 2026 · 8 min read · Zhang Peng, Founder
Back to Blog

Every year, buyers lose millions of dollars to Chinese suppliers who never deliver — or deliver something very different from what was agreed. The problem isn't that Alibaba is a scam. The problem is that no badge, rating, or Gold Supplier label tells you what actually happens inside the factory. This guide gives you a practical checklist to verify any Alibaba supplier before sending a single dollar.

Step 1 — Confirm the Business License Is Real

Every legitimate manufacturer in China must have a valid 营业执照 (business license). Ask the supplier to send you a clear photo or scan. Key things to check: the company name on the license should match the name on Alibaba and on the bank account they ask you to pay. The "经营范围" (business scope) should cover the product category you're buying. Cross-reference on China's National Enterprise Credit Information System (gsxt.samr.gov.cn) — it's public, free, and shows registration status.

If the supplier hesitates to share the license, or if the company name on the license doesn't match the Alibaba storefront, treat this as an immediate warning sign. Legitimate businesses have nothing to hide from a simple document request.

Step 2 — Request a Live Video Factory Tour

A real manufacturer will not refuse a live video call on WhatsApp or WeChat where they walk you through the production floor. Ask to see: the actual machines for your product, raw material storage, finished goods area, and packaging line. Refuse if they want to show you a pre-recorded video or a showroom only.

A supplier who "can't" do a live video from the factory is likely a trading company pretending to be a factory — or worse. During the call, ask them to show you the product being made and to hold up a piece of paper with today's date written on it. This simple check rules out recycled footage.

Step 3 — Verify with Third-Party Tools

China's government portal (aicr.samr.gov.cn) lets you search any company registration. For extra depth: Panjiva and ImportYeti show actual US import records — you can see if this supplier has shipped real goods to US buyers. Global Sources and Made-in-China also have independently verified supplier badges that are more rigorous than Alibaba's Gold Supplier tier.

If the supplier appears in import records with consistent shipments to established US companies, that's a strong positive signal. If they have zero import history despite claiming years of experience, probe deeper before proceeding.

Step 4 — Order a Sample — and Inspect It Properly

Requesting a sample is standard, but here's what most buyers miss: the sample is almost always made by hand, not by the production line. It will look better, fit better, and feel better than the bulk order. Ask for a "production sample" — a unit made by the actual factory worker on the actual line, not the R&D team.

Better still, have someone inspect the production line mid-order. A during-production inspection (when 20–40% of goods are complete) catches problems before they multiply to the full batch.

Step 5 — Check Payment Terms

No legitimate factory requires 100% upfront payment from a new buyer. Standard terms are 30% deposit, 70% before shipment — and even that 70% should only be paid after you or a third party has seen and approved the goods. Factories that insist on Western Union, cryptocurrency, or personal bank accounts are an immediate red flag.

If a supplier suddenly changes the bank account details at the last moment (a tactic called Business Email Compromise), verify directly by phone before transferring any funds. This single scam costs international buyers hundreds of millions annually.

Step 6 — Read Reviews Critically

Alibaba feedback is easy to game. Instead, search the supplier's exact company name plus "review," "complaint," or "scam" on Google. Check Trustpilot, Reddit's r/Entrepreneur, and industry-specific forums. One unresolved complaint is worth more than 50 generic 5-star reviews.

Also look at the review dates on Alibaba itself. A supplier with 200 reviews clustered in a 3-month window several years ago, and nothing since, may have purchased those reviews and is no longer actively trading.

Step 7 — Run a Pre-Shipment Inspection

Before the shipment leaves China, hire an independent inspector (or use a local sourcing agent) to physically check the goods at the factory. They verify quantity, quality, packaging, and compliance with your specs. This single step eliminates 90% of "not what I ordered" disputes.

Companies like SGS, Intertek, Bureau Veritas, QIMA, and Asia Inspection all operate in China and typically charge $200–$350 per man-day. For most orders, one man-day is sufficient. Compare that to the cost of a rejected container — it's not a difficult calculation.
