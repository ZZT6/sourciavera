Inside a Chinese Factory's Visitor Playbook — And the Line That Gives Away a Trading Company

Sep 14, 2026 · 7 min read · Zhang Peng, Founder
Back to Blog

Someone in the industry shared an actual internal training document with me recently — a step-by-step playbook a factory uses to receive overseas buyers who fly in for a visit. It covers everything from researching a buyer's company before they land, to the exact small-talk questions staff are told to ask in the car from the airport. Most of it is standard hospitality, useful to know if you're planning your own trip. One line in it, buried in the prep-work section, is worth more to a buyer than the rest of the document combined.

What a Good Host Does Before You Even Land

The playbook starts before your plane touches down. Staff are told to research the visiting company's size, products, and target markets, and to look up the visiting individual's job title and the purpose of their trip. They pull the specific samples a buyer asked about at a trade show and test them in advance, so nothing embarrassing happens in the room. They build a written itinerary and share it ahead of time — not to be polite, but because a schedule signals the company is running the visit, not improvising it. They also ask, in advance, whether you eat spicy food, whether you drink, and what kind of restaurant you'd prefer.

None of this proves a company is a real manufacturer. But a company that skips all of it — that can't tell you what to expect, hasn't tested the sample you asked about, and is figuring out dinner on the fly — is telling you something about how much they've done this before, and how seriously they take your order specifically.

The Ride From the Airport Isn't Just Small Talk

The playbook scripts the car ride in both directions. On the way to the hotel, it's warm and generic: how was your flight, is this your first trip to China, what do you like to do in your free time. But it also scripts questions aimed squarely at your business: what's the purpose of this trip, which other companies are you visiting, how do you normally reach your customers, what are your sales channels. This isn't idle curiosity — it's the host gathering intelligence on you as a buyer, the same way you're sizing them up as a supplier.

Knowing this is scripted doesn't mean you should stop making conversation. It means you can expect it, and it's worth noticing how specific and informed their questions are — a host who asks sharp, relevant questions about your market has clearly done this before with buyers like you.

The Welcome Ceremony Is Real, and It's Also a Performance

Flowers at arrival. Photos together. Business cards exchanged. A gift. A warm greeting meant to make you feel valued. This part of the playbook works, and it's not fake — most hosts genuinely want your visit to go well. But it's worth being clear-eyed about what it does and doesn't tell you: a trading company with a rented showroom and a well-trained receptionist can perform this exact sequence just as smoothly as a real 200-person factory. The warmth of the welcome has never once told a buyer who's actually running the production line.

The one line in the whole document that stopped me: under meeting preparation, hosts are told — if the company is a trading company, have your explanation ready in advance for why the office and the factory aren't in the same location.

The Line They Rehearse in Advance

Read that again. It's not an improvised answer to an awkward question — it's a prepared talking point, written into a training document, for a question buyers are expected to ask. That tells you two things at once. First, the office-factory mismatch comes up often enough that companies train staff on how to handle it, which means it's a real and common pattern worth watching for. Second — and this is the part that matters for how you act on it — a smooth, confident answer to "why isn't your office at the factory" proves nothing either way. It might be a completely reasonable answer from a real manufacturer with a separate sales office. It might also be a rehearsed line from a trading company that has never operated a production line in its life. The delivery will sound identical either way, because it was designed to.

The useful move isn't to accept or reject the explanation on the spot. It's to follow it with something a script can't fully cover: ask to see the specific production line for your product right then, not later. Ask a technical question about the process that only someone who runs it daily could answer without hesitating. We go into this in more detail in 10 things to check before paying a supplier deposit — the office-location question is really just one entry point into the larger factory-versus-trading-company check.

They Have a Question List for You, Too

The playbook includes a whole section of questions hosts are trained to ask about your company: your history, your business philosophy, your target customers, your sales channels. It's easy to read this as one-sided intelligence gathering, but it cuts both ways. A factory that bothers to learn who you are and how you sell is a factory that's selective about who it works with — which, counterintuitively, is a better sign than a supplier who asks nothing and would clearly take any order from anyone. Being interviewed a little is not a bad sign. Being interviewed by someone with obviously specific, informed questions about your market is a better one.

What the Choreography Can't Tell You

None of this — the flowers, the itinerary, the rehearsed line about the office, the sharp questions about your sales channels — actually confirms who is making your product. The whole performance can be identical whether you're standing in a real factory or a trading company's rented showroom with a meeting room built for exactly this purpose. Knowing the playbook doesn't replace verification. It just means you'll recognize the moments where a scripted answer is doing the work that an actual walk through the production floor should be doing instead.
