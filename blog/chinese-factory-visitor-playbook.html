Inside a Chinese Factory's Visitor Playbook — And the Line That Gives Away a Trading Company

Factory Visits
SourciaVera TeamSep 13, 2026 · Updated Oct 16, 2026 · 7 min read
Back to Blog

Most buyers come home from a factory visit in China and judge the supplier by how well they were treated. I think that's backwards. Hospitality is the most rehearsed part of the whole trip, and it tells you almost nothing about who actually makes your product.

I can say that with some confidence because someone in the industry shared an actual internal training document with me: a step-by-step playbook a factory uses to receive overseas buyers who fly in. I'm laying it out here close to how it was written, because the detail is the point. This is what a company that takes exporting seriously does before, during, and after your visit. Read it as a checklist, and then read my notes on which parts can be faked.

The short answer: a well-prepared Chinese supplier runs your visit from a script: research on you, a fixed itinerary, pickup, flowers and a group photo, a polished presentation. A trading company with a rented showroom can perform every step of it. What no script covers is a live look at your product's production line and process questions answered without hesitation, so judge those, not the welcome.

Why Does the Welcome Tell You So Little?

Three reasons:

  • Hospitality is the most rehearsed part of the trip. The visit runs from a script: research on you, a fixed itinerary, pickup, flowers, a group photo and a polished presentation.
  • A trading company can perform every step. A showroom can just as easily belong to a trading company reselling the output of several factories.
  • What no script covers is the production line. A live look at your product's line and process questions answered without hesitation are what separate a factory from a performance.

How Do Chinese Factories Prepare for a Buyer's Visit?

The playbook starts well before your plane lands. A proper host is expected to:

  • Research your company's size, products, and the markets you sell into
  • Look up the visiting individual's job title and the actual purpose of the trip
  • Pull the specific samples you asked about at a trade show and test them in advance, so nothing breaks or misbehaves in the room
  • Build a written, day-by-day itinerary and share it with you ahead of time, not as a courtesy, but because a schedule signals the company is running your visit rather than improvising it
  • Ask in advance whether you eat spicy food, whether you drink, and what kind of restaurant you'd prefer, so dinner isn't a debate in the car

By the time you land, the meeting room should already be set: snacks, a small gift, meeting documents, a voice recorder, a projector, and a product catalog with a pen for every person in your delegation.

What Order Does the Visit Day Follow?

The playbook sets a specific order for the day: presentation → factory tour → restaurant arrangement → a proper thank-you before you leave. For an important client, a dedicated pickup car is arranged in advance. Every part of the day is treated as a chance to show the company is serious about the relationship, not just the order in front of it.

What Happens at Pickup?

  • Someone meets you and offers to carry your luggage
  • They ask how your flight was, whether it was comfortable
  • A warm, almost ceremonial greeting is standard, something close to "it's always a pleasure to welcome a friend who has traveled from afar"
  • A car is already waiting outside; your bags go in the trunk, and you're shown to the back seat

Why Are They Asking So Many Questions in the Car?

Staff are scripted to work through specific questions in the car, roughly in this order: how the trip feels so far and whether it's your first time back in China since COVID, the real purpose of the trip and which other companies you're visiting, whether you travel to China on business often, what you like to do in your free time, and your opinion on recent news, a deliberately open, low-stakes way to keep things moving. None of this is idle curiosity. The host is forming a read on you as a buyer at the same moment you're forming a read on them as a supplier.

A good host also keeps you informed instead of leaving you guessing: what time you'll reach the hotel and that you're free to rest, what time they'll come by that afternoon to bring you to the office, and, on a multi-day trip, what time a driver will wait in your hotel lobby the next morning and which flight you're being taken to. They'll ask how many days you're staying and whether you have extra time to see anything else.

Does a Warm Welcome Prove It's a Real Factory?

A proper welcome looks like this: flowers on arrival, a group photo, a small gift, an exchange of business cards, and a warm greeting that makes it unmistakable you're being taken seriously. This part of the playbook is genuine; most hosts do want your visit to go well. But notice that it's entirely performable by a company that has never run a production line. A trading company with a rented showroom and a well-briefed receptionist can execute this sequence as smoothly as a real 200-person factory. Warmth has never told a buyer who's actually making the product.

How Does the Meeting Open?

Phones go on silent. The host waits for a clear moment to announce the meeting has started, introduces everyone in the room by name and role, and walks you through the day's plan with an itinerary sheet in hand. Then comes the company introduction, typically a short promotional video followed by a PPT that leans on brand history and technical capability.

The one line in the whole document that stopped me: under meeting preparation, hosts are told — if the company is a trading company, have your explanation ready in advance for why the office and the factory aren't in the same location.

"Why Isn't Your Office at the Factory?" The Answer Is Rehearsed

Read that again. It isn't an improvised answer to an awkward question. It's a prepared talking point, written into a training document, for a question buyers are expected to ask. That tells me two things. First, the office-factory mismatch comes up often enough that companies train staff to handle it, so it's a real and common pattern worth watching for. Second, and this is what matters for how you act, a smooth, confident answer to "why isn't your office at the factory" proves nothing either way. It might be a perfectly reasonable answer from a real manufacturer with a separate sales office. It might be a rehearsed line from a trading company that has never run a production line. The delivery sounds identical, because it was designed to.

So don't accept or reject the explanation on the spot. Follow it with something a script can't cover: ask to see the specific production line for your product right then, not later. Ask a technical question about the process that only someone who runs it daily could answer without hesitating. I go into this in more detail in 10 things to check before paying a supplier deposit; the office-location question is just one entry point into the larger factory-versus-trading-company check.

What Happens During the Meeting?

The host briefly explains the agenda and how long it should run. Before the presentation, they'll usually say something like "please feel free to interrupt if you have any questions." Afterward, they circle back with "is there anything else you'd like to ask about what we just covered?", a deliberate open door, not just politeness.

What Will the Factory Ask About Your Business?

Hosts are trained to ask about your company as thoroughly as you're learning about theirs: your background and history, your main products and services, your business philosophy, your target customers, and the sales channels you use to reach them. It's easy to read this as one-sided information gathering, but I see it differently. A factory that bothers to learn how you actually sell is a factory that's selective about who it works with, which, counterintuitively, is a better sign than a supplier who asks nothing and would clearly take an order from anyone.

Summary: Enjoy the Welcome, Then Judge the Production Floor

A welcome like this, with flowers, a group photo and a polished showroom, tells you how well a supplier hosts visitors, not what stands behind it. A showroom can just as easily belong to a trading company reselling output from several factories under one brand name, so ask to see the production line itself.

That's what stays with me. The playbook works precisely because it was never really about the factory. It's about the visit. The takeaway: enjoy the welcome, be a good guest, and then judge the production floor, not the flowers on the conference table.

In short, judge the production floor, not the flowers.
