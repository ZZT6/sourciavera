Inside a Chinese Factory's Visitor Playbook — And the Line That Gives Away a Trading Company

Sep 13, 2026 · 7 min read · Zhang Peng, Founder
Back to Blog

Someone in the industry shared an actual internal training document with me — a step-by-step playbook a factory uses to receive overseas buyers who fly in for a visit. I'm laying out the whole thing here, close to how it was written, because the detail is the point: this is what a company that takes exporting seriously actually does before, during, and after your visit. Read it as a checklist. If your own trip doesn't look like this, that's worth noticing — and if it does, you'll know exactly what it means.

Before You Even Book Your Flight, They're Already Working

The playbook starts well before your plane lands. A proper host is expected to:

  • Research your company's size, products, and the markets you sell into
  • Look up the visiting individual's job title and the actual purpose of the trip
  • Pull the specific samples you asked about at a trade show and test them in advance, so nothing breaks or misbehaves in the room
  • Build a written, day-by-day itinerary and share it with you ahead of time — not as a courtesy, but because a schedule signals the company is running your visit, not improvising it
  • Ask in advance whether you eat spicy food, whether you drink, and what kind of restaurant you'd prefer, so dinner isn't a debate happening in the car

By the time you land, the meeting room itself should already be set: snacks, a small gift, meeting documents, a voice recorder, a projector — and a product catalog with a pen laid out for every person in your delegation.

The Day Follows a Fixed Sequence

The playbook lays out a specific order for the day: presentation → factory tour → restaurant arrangement → a proper thank-you before you leave. For an important client, a dedicated pickup car is arranged in advance — every part of the day is treated as a chance to demonstrate the company is serious about the relationship, not just the order in front of it.

Pickup: What a Good Host Says and Does

  • Someone meets you and offers to carry your luggage
  • They ask how your flight was, whether it was comfortable
  • A warm, almost ceremonial greeting is standard — something close to "it's always a pleasure to welcome a friend who has traveled from afar"
  • A car is already waiting outside; your bags go in the trunk, and you're shown to the back seat

The Ride Isn't Just Small Talk

Staff are scripted to work through a specific set of questions in the car, roughly in this order: how the trip feels so far and whether it's your first time back in China since COVID, what the real purpose of the trip is and which other companies you're also visiting, whether you travel to China on business often, what you like to do in your free time, and your opinion on recent news — a deliberately open, low-stakes way to keep the conversation moving. None of this is idle curiosity. The host is forming a read on you as a buyer at exactly the same moment you're forming a read on them as a supplier.

Along the way, a good host also keeps you informed instead of leaving you guessing: what time you'll reach the hotel and that you're free to rest once you're there, what time they'll come by that afternoon to bring you to the office, and — on a multi-day trip — what time a driver will be waiting in your hotel lobby the next morning and which flight you're being taken to catch. They'll ask directly how many days you're staying and whether you have extra time to see anything else while you're here.

Arrival at the Factory: The Welcome

A proper welcome looks like this: flowers on arrival, a group photo, a small gift, an exchange of business cards, and a warm greeting meant to make it unmistakable that you're being taken seriously — not just processed. This part of the playbook is genuine; most hosts do want your visit to go well. It's also, worth noting, entirely performable by a company that has never run a production line. A trading company with a rented showroom and a well-briefed receptionist can execute this exact sequence just as smoothly as a real 200-person factory. Warmth has never once told a buyer who's actually making the product.

Before the Meeting Starts

Phones go on silent. Whoever is hosting waits for a clear moment to announce the meeting has started, introduces everyone in the room by name and role, and walks you through the day's plan with an itinerary sheet in hand. Then comes the company introduction — typically a short promotional video, followed by a PPT that leans on brand history and technical capability.

The one line in the whole document that stopped me: under meeting preparation, hosts are told — if the company is a trading company, have your explanation ready in advance for why the office and the factory aren't in the same location.

The Line They Rehearse in Advance

Read that again. It's not an improvised answer to an awkward question — it's a prepared talking point, written into a training document, for a question buyers are expected to ask. That tells you two things at once. First, the office-factory mismatch comes up often enough that companies train staff on how to handle it, which means it's a real and common pattern worth watching for. Second — and this is the part that matters for how you act on it — a smooth, confident answer to "why isn't your office at the factory" proves nothing either way. It might be a completely reasonable answer from a real manufacturer with a separate sales office. It might also be a rehearsed line from a trading company that has never operated a production line in its life. The delivery sounds identical either way, because it was designed to.

The useful move isn't to accept or reject the explanation on the spot. It's to follow it with something a script can't fully cover: ask to see the specific production line for your product right then, not later. Ask a technical question about the process that only someone who runs it daily could answer without hesitating. We go into this in more detail in 10 things to check before paying a supplier deposit — the office-location question is really just one entry point into the larger factory-versus-trading-company check.

During the Meeting Itself

The host briefly explains the agenda and roughly how long it should run. Before the presentation, they'll typically say something close to "please feel free to interrupt if you have any questions." Afterward, they circle back with "is there anything else you'd like to ask about what we just covered?" — a deliberate open door, not just politeness.

They Have a Question List for You, Too

Hosts are trained to ask about your company just as thoroughly as you're learning about theirs: your company's background and history, your main products and services, your business philosophy, who your target customers are, and which sales channels you use to reach them. It's easy to read this as one-sided information gathering, but it runs both ways. A factory that bothers to learn how you actually sell is a factory that's selective about who it works with — which, counterintuitively, is a better sign than a supplier who asks nothing and would clearly take an order from anyone.

What All of This Can (and Can't) Tell You

We've sat through this exact welcome — flowers, group photo, the works — in a Foshan showroom that turned out to belong to a trading company reselling output from three different factories under one brand name. The reception was better than some real factories manage; the coffee was better too. That's the part worth remembering: the playbook works precisely because it was never really about the factory. It's about the visit. Judge the production floor, not the flowers on the conference table.
