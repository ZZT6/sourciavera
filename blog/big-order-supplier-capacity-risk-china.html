The $480,000 Order That Nearly Bankrupted an Exporter

Real Case
SourciaVera TeamJun 22, 2026 · Updated Oct 24, 2026 · 8 min read

In short: a Chinese exporter accepted an order ten times larger than usual. He expected to make RMB 460,000 and ended up with RMB 80,000, a lost client and almost no cash. When a supplier accepts more than it can produce, the buyer usually ends up paying for it: delays, mid-order price changes, unannounced subcontracting and falling quality.

At SourciaVera we believe trust is built by showing the hard side of this business too. So here is a true story from a friend of ours in international trade. No names, but every number is real.

The story: an order that looked like the opportunity of the year

In late 2021, a German client placed an order for outdoor furniture worth USD 480,000. For context:

  • His annual revenue was around USD 2 million.
  • His normal orders were USD 30,000–50,000.
  • The largest order he had ever handled was USD 80,000.

He did the math: roughly RMB 460,000 in profit, about half a year's earnings. He said yes.

Week 3: the factory raises the price

The factory told him aluminum tube prices had gone up and it needed to raise its price by 8%. Expected profit dropped from RMB 460,000 to 230,000.

Week 6: the factory didn't have the capacity

When he went to check production, only 600 of the planned 2,400 sets were finished. The factory had taken on other clients' orders and was overloaded. To avoid further delay, he moved 800 sets to a second factory, which charged 12% more. Profit fell to RMB 150,000.

Then: quality problems from rushing

Under pressure to catch up, production sped up and welding defects appeared. The defect rate reached 15%, and about 240 units had to be reworked. Another week and a half lost.

The hardest blow: the money ran out

Between factory deposits and extra costs, he had advanced nearly RMB 1 million. He borrowed another RMB 200,000 and drew RMB 150,000 on credit. The German client would pay the balance after shipment, against the bill of lading. Factories and wages had to be paid, but the client's money hadn't arrived. He had a huge order and almost no cash.

Four months later: the real cost

  • The order shipped 18 days late and the client imposed a USD 9,600 penalty.
  • Final profit: RMB 80,000, instead of 460,000.
  • During those months he neglected his other clients and lost a three-year client worth USD 80,000–100,000 a year, plus two potential new ones.

Why this matters if you are the buyer

This is the exporter's side of the story, but every one of his problems has a buyer's side:

What the exporter went throughWhat the buyer goes through
The factory raised prices after the depositYour supplier asks to “adjust” the price mid-order
The factory took more orders than it could produceYour delivery date keeps moving
Part of the order went to another factoryYou receive goods from a factory you never verified
Production was rushed to catch upMore defects, right in your order
No cash to finish the jobThe supplier pushes for early payment or cuts material

None of this shows up in a quotation or a video call. It shows up on the factory floor.

How to protect yourself: 5 things to check before and during your order

  1. Lock the price in the contract. Agree in writing whether, and under what conditions, the price can change because of raw materials. If it isn't in the contract, any “adjustment” is a negotiation, not an imposition.
  2. Verify real capacity, not the sales pitch. Ask for monthly capacity and current order load, then verify on site: active lines, shifts, raw material in stock. This is exactly what we check in a factory verification.
  3. Prohibit unannounced subcontracting. State in the contract that no part of the order may move to another factory without your approval. If it does, you want to know which one, and verify it too.
  4. Inspect during production, not just at the end. An inspection at 20–30% completion catches delays and defects while there is still time to fix them. If you only check before shipment, it's too late. See our inspection standards and what a pre-shipment inspection costs.
  5. Stage payments and tie them to inspections. Split payments, and for large orders, deliveries too. Release each payment only when that stage passes inspection (more in how to pay a Chinese supplier and 10 things to check before paying a deposit).

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does a supplier taking a very large order put the buyer at risk?

A supplier that accepts more than it can produce tends to push the problems onto its customers: delivery dates move, prices are renegotiated mid-order, parts of the order go to other factories without notice and quality drops when production is rushed.

How can I check a factory's real capacity?

Ask for monthly capacity and the current order load, then verify on site: active production lines, shifts, raw material in stock and the work in progress for other customers. A video call or a quotation cannot show you this.

When should I inspect a large order?

During production, not only before shipment. An inspection when 20 to 30 percent of the order is made shows delays and defects while there is still time to fix them, and payments can be tied to each inspection.

Should my contract forbid subcontracting?

Yes, state that no part of the order may move to another factory without your written approval. If it does move, ask which factory it is and have that one verified as well.

Conclusion

A big order isn't bad in itself. The danger is accepting one, or handing one to a supplier, without checking three things:

  • Cash flow: is there enough money to carry the operation until the final payment?
  • Production capacity: can the factory meet deadlines and quality while running other orders?
  • Management capacity: are there enough people and time to control it without neglecting everything else?

Our friend paid RMB 80,000, and a three-year client, for this lesson. If your next order in China is bigger than usual, for you or for your supplier, verify before you pay the deposit.
