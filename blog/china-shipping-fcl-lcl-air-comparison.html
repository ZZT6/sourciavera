Shipping from China: FCL, LCL, or Air — How to Choose Based on Time and Volume

Export Logistics
SourciaVera TeamSep 20, 2026 · Updated Oct 24, 2026 · 5 min read

Who should choose your freight mode: you or your supplier?

Most buyers let the supplier pick the freight mode — whichever is easiest for the supplier to arrange. That's a mistake. Easiest for the supplier isn't cheapest or fastest for you, and this is one of the few decisions in an order where you can hold all the information yourself.

The short answer: choose by your real volume and deadline. As a rule of thumb, FCL makes sense from about 15 CBM up, LCL between 1 and 15 CBM or when you are combining several suppliers, and air freight under 150–200 kg when the cargo is genuinely urgent. Whatever the mode, plan around total lead time — consolidation, customs and last-mile delivery included — not ocean or flight time alone.

Get your own numbers first: actual CBM and weight — not the supplier's estimate — your real delivery deadline, and whether this shipment is combining with other suppliers' cargo. Compare FCL, LCL, and air against those numbers on transit time, cost per cubic meter, and consolidation risk. Decide in advance what delay you can absorb, before you're negotiating that answer under pressure with a container already at origin.

Buyer evidence

The actual CBM, gross weight, and hard delivery deadline — not the "ideal" date the supplier quotes.

A typical scenario: several suppliers, several small shipments

The problem: a common pattern. Two or three suppliers finish around the same time, each books its own shipment, and the buyer gets separate small shipments on different dates with updates from different agents.

The takeaway: decide the mode yourself, on combined volume and real deadline, and consolidate at origin when it makes sense.

FCL vs. LCL vs. air: quick rules

  • FCL makes sense from 15 CBM up, or when the per-volume cost of LCL stops dropping.
  • LCL makes sense between 1 and 15 CBM, or when the order combines multiple suppliers.
  • Air makes sense under 150–200 kg with a real urgency, or for samples and first lots.
  • Total lead time is not just ocean transit — add consolidation, customs, and last-mile delivery.

Decision table: transit time, cost and risk by mode

Mode Typical volume Estimated port-to-port time When it makes sense
FCL (full container load) 15+ CBM 25–40 days depending on destination Large single-supplier orders
LCL (less than container load) 1–15 CBM 30–45 days (includes consolidation and deconsolidation) Small orders or multi-supplier shipments
Air freight Under 150–200 kg 5–10 days Urgent orders, samples, first lots

Transit times above are industry reference ranges for routes from mainland China. Actual time varies by origin port, final destination, sailing frequency, and customs conditions. Check with your freight forwarder for current estimates on your specific route.

Total lead time

The real sum of production, origin consolidation, transit, destination customs, and last-mile delivery — not just the time the ship or plane is in the air.

As a rough guide (typical ranges, not one specific order), production runs 25–35 days and ocean freight to most Latin American ports adds another 30–45. The takeaway: that's two to three months of financial commitment — the number to plan around, not the sailing time alone.

What should you confirm before booking freight?

  • Confirm the actual CBM and weight of the order — not the supplier's estimate.
  • Clarify whether the shipment combines more than one supplier. If it does, decide on the combined volume: consolidated cargo can still fill a full container.
  • Ask how many days consolidation takes before the container departs.
  • Build in extra time if your destination requires additional customs documentation. For the late-October 2026 China-to-US ocean and air picture, see what to confirm with your supplier and forwarder.

Frequently asked questions

Is LCL always slower than FCL?

Generally yes, because it adds consolidation and deconsolidation time at both ends — though the gap is often days, not weeks. The real variable is usually how often consolidated sailings leave for your destination port.

When does it make sense to pay more for air freight?

When the cost of a delay — missed selling season, customer penalty, stockout — exceeds the price difference versus ocean. For most products, that threshold is crossed quickly on urgent purchases under 150 kg.

Can I mix modes in a single order?

Yes. It's common to send part of an order by air to protect a launch date and the rest by sea to reduce total cost. This requires coordinating production and consolidation timelines for both parts from the origin.

Summary: Make the Freight Decision Yourself

Freight rarely goes wrong because someone picked the "wrong" mode on paper. It goes wrong because nobody on the buyer's side actually made the decision. Buyers with predictable deliveries know their CBM, their deadline, and how much delay they can absorb.
