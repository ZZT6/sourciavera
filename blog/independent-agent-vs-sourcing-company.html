Independent Sourcing Agent vs Sourcing Company in China: Which Fits Your Order?

By Zhang Peng (Miguel Lopez), Founder · Jul 21, 2026 · Updated Oct 16, 2026 · 5 min read

Independent agent or sourcing company: what most buyers get wrong

Most buyers frame this as "one person or a company?" and then compare fees. I think that's the wrong comparison. An independent agent and a sourcing company can quote the same price for the same order and still leave you in very different positions the day something goes wrong. What actually separates them is who's accountable in writing, how visible the real factory is behind your order, and whether evidence lands in your inbox at each stage or you're just told things over a call.

Both options can work well. The question isn't which is "better" in the abstract; it's which fits the size and risk of the order in front of you, and what happens if that order grows.

The short answer: an independent agent usually suits small, low-risk trial orders where direct contact matters most. A sourcing company suits larger or growing orders that need backup if one person is unavailable. Either way, choose the partner that gives you one named, accountable contact and documented evidence from the factory at every stage.

What goes wrong when a trial order scales up?

Here's a common pattern. The problem: a buyer starts with a small trial order through one agent. The trial goes fine. Then the order grows into real volume, production gets tight, and the agent is suddenly hard to reach, with no one else who knows the order. Other buyers go the opposite way: they hire a larger company for the backup and end up passed between account managers, never sure who has actually been to the factory. The takeaway: neither chose badly. They just didn't decide upfront how much backup they'd need if things scaled, which is exactly where the gap shows up.

Agent vs sourcing company at a glance

  • An independent agent typically offers direct contact, but with less backup if volume grows or the agent is unavailable.
  • A sourcing company offers more structural backup, but the risk is losing direct contact if too many layers sit between you and whoever executes.
  • What actually matters is not the organization's size — it's whether you have a clear, named point of contact with documented evidence at every stage.
  • Always ask who specifically visits the factory — not just who signs the contract.

How do the two models compare on contact, backup and evidence?

Criterion Independent agent Sourcing company
Direct contact Generally high Can be diluted across management layers
Backup if agent unavailable Low — depends on one person Higher — team behind the service
Visibility of the real supplier Depends on the agent's transparency Depends on whether the company shares or hides the factory contact
Evidence delivered Varies widely by agent Should be more consistent if the process is well-defined
Named point of contact

An identified person, with a documented communication history, who answers specifically for your order — whether they work alone or within a team.

What should you ask before choosing?

  • Ask who exactly will be your contact and whether that person changes during the order.
  • Request evidence from a prior order similar to yours (verification report, production photos, shipping document).
  • Confirm whether you will have direct access to the factory contact, or whether everything passes exclusively through the intermediary.
  • Decide in advance what level of backup you need if your order grows from a small trial to a larger volume.

Frequently asked questions

Is an independent agent always cheaper than a sourcing company?

Not necessarily. Pricing depends on the scope of service, not on whether you work with one person or a team. An experienced independent agent may charge more than a limited-scope company service.

What happens if my independent agent is unavailable during a production crisis?

That's the main risk of depending on a single person without backup. It's worth asking this before committing a large order, especially if the production timeline has little slack.

Does a sourcing company always mean losing direct contact?

Not necessarily. It depends on how the team is structured — some keep a dedicated account owner per client rather than rotating contacts based on internal availability.

Summary: Ask Who Answers for the Order and Who Goes to the Factory

Buyers who end up in trouble rarely picked the "wrong" type of partner. They picked without asking the two questions that matter: who, by name, answers for my order, and who actually goes to the factory? If you get clear answers to both, in writing, the size of the organization behind them matters much less than people think.

To sum up, the size of the organization matters less than two answers in writing: who, by name, answers for your order, and who actually goes to the factory. If you pick without asking, a trial order that grows can leave you with an agent who is hard to reach and nobody else who knows the order, or with a chain of account managers.
