5 Things a Live Video Call Can Reveal That Factory Photos Can't

Verification
SourciaVera TeamAug 14, 2026 · Updated Oct 16, 2026 · 6 min read
Back to Blog

Most buyers think getting a supplier on a video call is the check. It isn't. A video call is only as good as what you ask to see during it — and I've found that most calls turn into a polite screen tour where the buyer nods along and the supplier decides what's on camera.

The short answer: a live video call can reveal what factory photos can't — whether the scale matches the claim, whether the machines fit your product, whether the feed is really live, whether the rep knows production, and whether "in stock" stock exists. It only works if you ask for specific things on camera instead of accepting the supplier's tour.

Photos can be staged, borrowed from a competitor, or years out of date, so a live call is genuinely harder to fake. But only if you use it to check something specific. These 5 checks are how I'd turn a video call from small talk into real evidence. The “typical case” examples under each check are illustrative, not accounts of specific clients, and the amounts in them are examples.

Why Is a Live Call Better Evidence Than Photos?

Photos can be staged, borrowed from a competitor or years out of date, so a live call is genuinely harder to fake. It is only evidence, though, if you ask for something specific. Left to the supplier, most calls become a polite screen tour where the supplier decides what is on camera.

1. Does the Factory's Scale Match Its Claims?

A "200-worker factory" should visibly have workstations, not one busy corner filmed at a flattering angle.

A typical case: a buyer sourcing 500 sets of 18-inch alloy wheel rims for a used-car export business has a supplier whose Alibaba page lists "200+ employees, own casting and CNC facility."

What the call shows: the rep walks maybe fifteen meters, past two polishing stations and a stack of finished rims, then says the rest of the floor is "in another building, closed today." No CNC lathe ever appears on camera — the machine that actually cuts a wheel's offset and bore to spec. A factory that casts and finishes its own rims has that machine in plain sight; a reseller repackaging generic wheels doesn't need one.

The outcome: the buyer stops at that call, and later finds the same rim listed under two other company names at two different prices — the usual sign of a trading company moving someone else's stock.

The takeaway: ask to see the one machine your product can't be made without.

You Can Check

On the call, ask them to walk to the far end of the production floor and count visible workstations as they go.

2. Do the Machines Actually Fit Your Product?

A factory that makes your product should be able to point a camera at a machine actually producing it — not a generic assembly line.

LED headlight assemblies are where this gap shows up most, because the number on the box and the number a headlight actually produces are often two different things.

A typical case: a supplier's spec sheet for a $28-per-unit LED headlight assembly claims 12,000 lumens — which turns out to be the raw output of the two LED chips inside, added together and rounded up, not a measurement of the finished, lensed assembly.

What the call shows: a buyer placing a 3,000-piece container order asks the rep to walk to wherever they test finished units and show a reading. There's no integrating sphere on the floor — the light-sealed chamber that measures true lumen output from an assembled housing — only a handheld lux meter aimed at a bare bulb from about a foot away, which reads brighter than reality. The rep switches to reciting the chip maker's datasheet.

The takeaway: that's the difference between a factory that engineers headlights and one that solders chips into a housing and copies the chip's spec sheet onto the retail box.

Ask them to show the specific machine or line producing your product type, live.

3. Is the "Live" Tour Really Live?

Pre-recorded "live tours" are common enough that a simple test is worth running every time.

Here is what the live version looks like. On a factory visit for a Guatemalan buyer, I walked the production hall in the middle of his night, sending photos and video as I went and calling him so he could watch live. I filmed more than the one unit we were quoting, so he could see what else the factory builds (full case).

Pre-recorded tours show up with auto parts too.

A typical case: a buyer sourcing polyurethane suspension bushings for three sedan models notices the factory's "live tour" looks identical to a clip already on the company's own YouTube channel.

What the check shows: asked to hold up a note with the date and a word the buyer picks on the spot, the rep hesitates, blames lag, and the feed cuts out for ninety seconds before resuming with the same walking pattern past the same molding machines. Asked to see the bushing molds for the bolt pattern and bore diameter in the PO, the rep points at a shelf of unlabeled molds and can't say which matches which application.

The takeaway: buyers of suspension and brake hardware run into this constantly — a mold built for one bolt pattern gets used for parts sold under several vehicle fitments, and the mismatch only shows up after installation.

You Can Check

Ask them to hold up a handwritten note with today's date and a word you choose on the spot.

4. Can the Rep Answer Questions Off the Script?

Sales pitches are rehearsed. Real production knowledge shows up when a question falls outside the script.

A typical case: an exhaust systems buyer placing a $40,000 order for mandrel-bent stainless exhaust kits asks the rep, mid-call, what wall thickness of tubing they're running and why.

What the question reveals: the rep, fluent and smooth up to that point, pauses and says he'll "check with the workshop and send it in chat." A production manager who actually runs mandrel-bending equipment answers in under five seconds, because wall thickness sets the bend radius and is dialed into the machine every shift.

The outcome: over the following messages it emerges that the company's Alibaba showroom also sells garden tools and lithium battery packs — a product spread that points strongly to a trading company, since real manufacturers tend to specialize in one process. The business license lists an office-unit address with no workshop at all.

The takeaway: one technical question off the script tells you more than a polished tour.

You Can Check

Ask something not in their usual pitch — a defect rate, a recent production issue, a technical spec.

5. Is "In Stock, Ships Immediately" Actually True?

"In stock, ready to ship" should be provable in about ten seconds on a video call.

A typical case: a buyer needs 1,200 sets of front brake calipers within three weeks for a seasonal parts-store order, and has been quoted "in stock, ship within 48 hours" in chat.

What the call shows: the rep first pulls up a product photo saved on his phone until the buyer asks to see the actual pallets. It takes over four minutes of walking, two phone calls off-camera, and a stop at a shelf holding maybe 80 units before anything resembling the calipers appears — nowhere near 1,200.

The outcome: the honest answer, which only comes out after the buyer pushes, is two to three weeks of production still ahead.

The takeaway: that's a fixable timeline problem if you know before wiring a deposit against a 48-hour promise; it's a much worse one if the seasonal order is late and the parts store has already committed shelf space.

You Can Check

Ask them to walk to the actual warehouse shelf holding your product — not a saved photo they already have.

Summary: Ask for Something Specific on Camera

None of these 5 checks needs special equipment or fluent Mandarin — just a willingness to ask for something specific instead of accepting a general tour. Suppliers who fake video calls are betting that buyers won't push past the polite version. What I've learned is that pushing costs you nothing, a real factory rarely minds, and how a supplier reacts to one unscripted request tells you almost everything.

In short, a video call is only as good as what you ask to see during it. If you accept the supplier's tour, you may pay a deposit against a factory, a stock level or a lead time that you have not actually seen.
