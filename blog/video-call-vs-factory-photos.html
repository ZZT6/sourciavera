5 Things a Live Video Call Can Reveal That Factory Photos Can't

Aug 14, 2026 · 6 min read · Zhang Peng, Founder
Back to Blog

Most buyers think getting a supplier on a video call is the check. It isn't. A video call is only as good as what you ask to see during it — and I've found that most calls turn into a polite screen tour where the buyer nods along and the supplier decides what's on camera.

Photos can be staged, borrowed from a competitor, or years out of date, so a live call is genuinely harder to fake. But only if you use it to check something specific. These 5 checks are how I'd turn a video call from small talk into real evidence.

1. Whether the Scale Matches the Claim

A "200-worker factory" should visibly have workstations, not one busy corner filmed at a flattering angle.

This came up almost word for word in a case from last year. A buyer sourcing 500 sets of 18-inch alloy wheel rims for a used-car export business had a supplier whose Alibaba page listed "200+ employees, own casting and CNC facility." On the video call, the rep walked maybe fifteen meters, passed two polishing stations and a stack of finished rims, then said the rest of the floor was "in another building, closed today." No CNC lathe ever appeared on camera — the machine that actually cuts a wheel's offset and bore to spec, not just polishes the face. A factory that casts and finishes its own rims has that machine in plain sight; a reseller repackaging generic wheels from three different casters doesn't, because it doesn't need one. The buyer never got past that call, and later found the same rim listed under two other company names at two different prices — the usual sign of a trading company moving someone else's stock.

You Can Check

On the call, ask them to walk to the far end of the production floor and count visible workstations as they go.

What I Verify On-Site

I physically walk the same floor and count equipment against the claimed capacity, with no camera angle to manage.

2. Whether Workers and Machines Match the Product Category

A factory that makes your product should be able to point a camera at a machine actually producing it — not a generic assembly line.

LED headlight assemblies are where this gap shows up most, because the number on the box and the number a headlight actually produces are often two different things. In a typical scenario, a supplier's spec sheet for a $28-per-unit LED headlight assembly claims 12,000 lumens — which turns out to be the raw output of the two LED chips inside, added together and rounded up, not a measurement of the finished, lensed assembly. A buyer placing a 3,000-piece container order asks the rep on camera to walk to wherever they test finished units and show a reading. There's no integrating sphere on the floor — the enclosed, light-sealed chamber that measures true lumen output from an assembled housing — only a handheld lux meter aimed at a bare bulb from about a foot away, which reads brighter than reality and can't test a sealed housing at all. The rep switches to reciting the chip maker's datasheet. That's the difference between a factory that engineers headlights and one that solders chips into a housing and copies the chip's spec sheet onto the retail box.

You Can Check

Ask them to show the specific machine or line producing your product type, live.

What I Verify On-Site

I identify equipment by make and model and confirm it's actually suited to your product's manufacturing process.

3. Proof It's Not Recorded Footage

Pre-recorded "live tours" are common enough that a simple test is worth running every time.

I've seen how far this goes. When I compared six container-home factories side by side for a buyer (the full case is here), the finalist filmed the specific line, a dated whiteboard sign, and that day's serial numbers on request. Two others sent a pre-recorded tour instead — and turned out to be the exact same factory floor, filmed from two different login accounts.

The same pattern shows up with auto parts. Picture a buyer sourcing polyurethane suspension bushings for three sedan models who notices the factory's "live tour" looks identical to a clip already on the company's own YouTube channel from eight months earlier. Asked to hold up a note with the date and the word "Toledo," the rep hesitates, blames lag, and the feed cuts out for ninety seconds before resuming with the same walking pattern past the same molding machines. The buyer then asks to see the bushing molds for the bolt pattern and bore diameter in the PO — the fixture that decides whether the part fits its mounting bracket. The rep points the camera at a shelf of unlabeled molds and can't say which matches which application. Buyers of suspension and brake hardware run into this constantly: a mold built for one bolt pattern gets used for parts sold under several vehicle fitments, and the mismatch only shows up after installation.

You Can Check

Ask them to hold up a handwritten note with today's date and a word you choose on the spot.

What I Verify On-Site

I record dated video during every visit as standard evidence included in your report.

4. How the Rep Handles Unscripted Questions

Sales pitches are rehearsed. Real production knowledge shows up when a question falls outside the script.

Consider an exhaust systems buyer placing a $40,000 order for mandrel-bent stainless exhaust kits who asks the rep, mid-call, what wall thickness of tubing they're running and why. The rep, who has narrated the tour smoothly in fluent English up to that point, pauses for several seconds and says he'll "check with the workshop and send it in chat." A production manager who actually runs mandrel-bending equipment answers that in under five seconds, because wall thickness sets the bend radius and is dialed into the machine every shift. Over the following messages it emerges that the company's Alibaba showroom also sells garden tools and lithium battery packs — a product spread that, on its own, points strongly to a trading company, since real manufacturers tend to specialize in one process. The business license, requested afterward, lists a "Room 6B" office address with no workshop unit number at all.

You Can Check

Ask something not in their usual pitch — a defect rate, a recent production issue, a technical spec.

What I Verify On-Site

I ask harder questions in Mandarin, in person, where evasive answers are easier to catch than on a translated video call.

5. Whether Inventory Matches "Ships Immediately" Claims

"In stock, ready to ship" should be provable in about ten seconds on a video call.

Take a buyer who needs 1,200 sets of front brake calipers within three weeks for a seasonal parts-store order, and has been quoted "in stock, ship within 48 hours" in chat. On the call, the rep first pulls up a product photo saved on his phone and starts describing it until the buyer asks to see the actual pallets. It takes over four minutes of walking, two phone calls to someone off-camera, and a stop at a shelf holding maybe 80 units before anything resembling the calipers appears — nowhere near 1,200. The honest answer, which only comes out after the buyer pushes, is two to three weeks of production still ahead. That's a fixable timeline problem if you know before wiring a deposit against a 48-hour promise; it's a much worse one if the seasonal order is late and the parts store has already committed shelf space.

You Can Check

Ask them to walk to the actual warehouse shelf holding your product — not a saved photo they already have.

What I Verify On-Site

I check physical stock counts against what's promised in the listing before you commit to a ship date.

What I've Learned From a Lot of Video Calls

None of these 5 checks needs special equipment or fluent Mandarin — just a willingness to ask for something specific instead of accepting a general tour. Suppliers who fake video calls are betting that buyers won't push past the polite version. What I've learned is that pushing costs you nothing, a real factory rarely minds, and how a supplier reacts to one unscripted request tells you almost everything.
