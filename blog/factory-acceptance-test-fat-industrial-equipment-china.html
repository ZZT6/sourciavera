Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) for Industrial Equipment in China

Industrial Machinery
SourciaVera TeamNov 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer: A Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) verifies electrical wiring, safety interlocks, and live running load at the Chinese manufacturer before crating. Unlike carton-level inspections, a machinery FAT catches voltage mismatches and mechanical flaws while the builder still has parts and technicians on site.

Why Standard Carton Inspections Fail for Industrial Machinery

A statistical Acceptable Quality Limit (AQL) check works well for packed boxes of consumer goods, but it does not prove that a shredder, CNC center, or packaging line runs safely under load. Once a 5-ton machine clears ocean freight and arrives at your U.S. or Australian facility, fixing a miswired PLC or wrong motor frequency costs multiples of an on-site pre-shipment test.

For buyers sourcing industrial equipment from China, scheduling a documented FAT before paying the final 70% balance aligns the test protocol with core international frameworks: IEC 60204-1 for machine electrical equipment, ISO 12100 for hazard labeling, ISO 13849-1 for safety control circuits, IEC 62381 for automation FAT/SAT workflows, and ISO 20816 for vibration limits.

6-Part FAT Checklist Before Crating and Final Payment

Every equipment FAT protocol should verify six technical stages before anyone nails the export crate shut:

  1. Engineering drawings & certificates: Match electrical schematics (IEC 60204-1), P&ID layouts, material test reports (MTRs), and PLC/HMI program revision backups against the physical machine.
  2. Mechanical build & nameplates: Confirm overall footprint, structural weld finishes, shaft alignment, lubrication points, and permanent machine nameplates with hazard labels (ISO 12100).
  3. Electrical safety & voltage verification: China's industrial grid is 380V 50Hz, while U.S. plants typically require 480V 60Hz (or 220V 60Hz). Verify control-cabinet IP sealing (IEC 60529), protective earth continuity, insulation testing, emergency stops, and lockout/tagout isolation points.
  4. Automation & PLC logic: Test all digital and analog I/O signals, HMI screen navigation, fault alarms, and manual-to-automatic mode transitions (IEC 62381).
  5. No-load and loaded endurance run: Run dry cycles first, then test with actual feedstock or load media while checking motor temperature, power draw, and bearing vibration (ISO 20816).
  6. Preservation, spare parts & punch-list sign-off: Confirm VCI rust protection, desiccant packs, internal transport locks, spare consumables, and written closure of every open punch-list item.

FAT vs. Pre-Shipment Inspection (PSI) vs. SAT

Industrial buyers often confuse a machinery FAT with a generic ISO 2859-1 AQL pre-shipment inspection. The table below clarifies where each verification step applies.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a FAT and a standard pre-shipment inspection?

A Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) verifies electrical safety, PLC logic, emergency stops, and sustained running performance under power before crating. A standard Pre-Shipment Inspection (PSI) uses ISO 2859-1 AQL sampling on finished batch goods and packaging rather than live industrial load testing.

Which international standards guide a machinery FAT in China?

Buyers commonly structure FAT protocols around IEC 60204-1 for machine electrical equipment, ISO 12100 for safety labeling and risk reduction, ISO 13849-1 for safety-related control circuits, IEC 62381 for automation FAT/SAT workflows, and ISO 20816 for mechanical vibration.

Why should voltage and frequency be checked during a Chinese machinery FAT?

China uses 380V 50Hz industrial power, whereas North American and many Latin American plants run 480V 60Hz or 220V 60Hz. Checking transformer ratings, motor nameplates, and control wiring under power before crating prevents costly rewiring after ocean delivery.

Can a buyer attend a Chinese factory FAT remotely?

Yes. An on-site representative can stream a live video session and deliver unedited footage showing serial plates, safety stops, power draw, and load runs—allowing your engineers to sign off on punch-list items before releasing the final balance.
