6 Signs Your "Factory" Is Actually a Trading Company in Disguise

Supplier Types
SourciaVera TeamAug 11, 2026 · Updated Oct 19, 2026 · 7 min read
Back to Blog

I'll say something that surprises a lot of buyers: I don't think buying from a trading company is a problem. Plenty of them run a legitimate business consolidating orders across factories. What I object to is paying factory price without knowing there's a middleman in between. Cutting one out can lower the price, but only if there's actually a factory behind the listing to cut it from.

The good news is that traders give themselves away long before the first quality problem, if you know what to look for. These are the six signs I watch for, each with a scenario of how it typically plays out, what you can check yourself, and what our team checks when we're standing on the floor.

The short answer: a supplier is probably a trading company when two or more of these show up together: it resists a short unannounced video walkthrough, its catalog spans unrelated categories, it can't state line capacity for your product, its lead time is too short for the volume, its price barely moves with quantity, or it can't answer process questions with real numbers.

Why Does It Matter Whether You Are Buying From a Trader?

Three reasons, all about money and control:

  • A margin you did not budget for. A trader's margin is paid by you. Paying factory price for it is the problem, not the trader itself.
  • Less control. A trader passes your questions to someone else, so answers on capacity, tolerances and lead times reach you second-hand.
  • The signs show early. Traders usually give themselves away long before the first quality problem, which is the cheapest moment to find out.

1. They Resist an Unannounced, Short Video Walkthrough

A scheduled tour a week out can be staged. A supplier who agrees on the spot to a 15-minute video call this week, without knowing exactly what you'll ask to see, almost always has nothing to hide, because they own the floor they're showing you. 2. The Catalog Spans Too Many Unrelated Categories

A single profile selling electronics, furniture, and auto parts isn't a specialized factory. It's someone reselling from multiple sources.

Here's how that typically looks. The problem: a buyer sourcing aftermarket alloy wheel rims requested the full product catalog from a supplier listed as a "wheel rim factory" on Alibaba, gold supplier badge included. The PDF that came back ran to 40 pages: rims on page one, then folding camping chairs, phone cases, and a run of USB power banks by page twelve. Nothing on those later pages shared a factory, a mold shop, or even a city with the rims.

In Chinese company registration, a business name containing 贸易 (trade) or 进出口 (import-export) normally indicates that trading is the main business, so check the business scope (经营范围) on the license to see whether manufacturing is listed. A catalog like that one often maps onto a trading structure: each category sourced from a different subcontractor, the product never touched. You can check this before any call is scheduled. What was checked: that buyer pulled the business license number off the Alibaba profile and ran it through a Chinese company registry lookup, which listed the registered scope as general trade, not automotive parts manufacturing. The takeaway: a five-minute license lookup settled what the badge hadn't.

You Can Check

Count how many unrelated categories appear in their Alibaba catalog.

3. They Can't Show Their Own Production Capacity

A real factory knows, without hesitating, how many lines and workers it has dedicated to your category.

A typical exchange: on a call about a brake caliper and suspension arm order, a buyer asked how many CNC machining centers and QC staff were dedicated to automotive components specifically, not the total headcount on the sales rep's business card. The first answer was "more than 50 workers." Asked again for lines instead of headcount, the number became "several," then, a few messages later, "it depends on the order."

A factory running its own machining floor for calipers and control arms can give a line count immediately, because it schedules those lines every morning. A supplier subcontracting the machining gives a headcount instead, because headcount is the only number that's actually theirs. The buyer's follow-up (which specific line was running the current order that week) got no answer at all, just a repeated offer to "check with the factory and get back to you." That's an odd sentence for someone claiming to be standing in that factory. The takeaway: ask for lines, not headcount.

4. Delivery Times Are Unusually Short for the Volume

If your order volume should take real production weeks but they offer near-immediate shipping, in my experience it's usually already sitting somewhere as someone else's inventory.

5. The Per-Unit Price Barely Changes Between Small and Large Orders

Real factory pricing has a shape. The steepest drop happens right at the MOQ threshold, where setup and tooling costs get absorbed; each volume tier after that gives a progressively smaller discount, not a flat, evenly spaced line. A supplier who can't explain the step, or just knocks a flat percentage off every quantity, usually isn't pricing off their own production costs.

A scenario I recognize: a buyer sourcing LED tail light assemblies got a quote of $8.40 per unit at 500 pieces and $8.10 per unit at 5,000 pieces, a 4% difference across a tenfold jump in volume. Asked to explain the curve, the supplier repeated that it was "our best price," without addressing tooling, injection-mold amortization, or the LED driver board's own volume pricing from its component supplier.

A real assembly line has fixed costs (the injection mold for the housing, the jig that positions the driver board) that get absorbed early and then largely stop moving the per-unit number after a few thousand units. Instead, this curve sat nearly flat across the whole range, which is what happens when a trading company applies a fixed percentage margin over whatever its subcontractor quoted at each tier. The takeaway: a flat price curve is a pricing signal, not a discount.

You Can Check

Request quotes at 3 different volumes and see if they can explain why the price changes the way it does, not just that it does.

6. They Can't Explain the Production Process in Technical Detail

Any salesperson can answer product questions. Only someone who runs the line can answer process questions.

Picture a buyer mid-negotiation on a batch of aftermarket wheel rims, asking what tolerance the CNC lathe held on the hub bore diameter, and whether it changed for a two-piece forged rim versus a one-piece cast one. The reply: "our quality is very good, tolerance is very tight, no problem." Pressed again for an actual number, the answer became "we can discuss during production."

A machinist running that lathe every day knows the tolerance in microns, because he sets the fixture to it every shift. That's why a real supplier gives a feature-specific number instead of a comfort phrase like "tighter than normal" or "no problem." When nobody who has touched the machine is on the call, the answer stays vague however many times you ask. On an order like forged wheels or brake components, that vagueness matters: a small tolerance misjudgment by a supplier can cost an importer thousands.

You Can Check

Ask a specific question about the manufacturing process itself, not the product, and judge whether the answer is specific or generic.

Summary: What to Do When Two or More Signs Appear

No single sign here is proof. Some trading companies are transparent, reliable partners who tell you exactly what they are, and I'm happy to see buyers work with them. The real risk is not knowing, and paying factory price for a relationship that's quietly costing you an extra margin you didn't budget for.

My rule of thumb: one sign is a question, two or more together are reason enough to confirm who you're actually dealing with before committing to the order. Buyers who get hurt rarely miss every sign. They see one or two, and decide not to ask.

If two or more of these signs appear together, confirm who you are dealing with before the deposit leaves your account. If you skip it, the realistic outcomes are a price that carries a margin you did not plan for, and a first order that depends on a factory you have never seen.
