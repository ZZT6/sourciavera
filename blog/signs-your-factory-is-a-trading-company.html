6 Signs Your "Factory" Is Actually a Trading Company in Disguise

Sep 4, 2026 · 7 min read · Zhang Peng, Founder
Back to Blog

Buying from a trading company isn't automatically a problem — plenty run a legitimate business consolidating orders across factories. The problem is paying factory price without knowing there's a middleman in between. Cutting one out typically saves 10-30% on standard products, and up to 50% on customized goods — but only if there's actually a factory behind the listing to cut it from. These 6 signs show up before you sign, not after the first quality problem.

1. They Resist an Unannounced, Short Video Walkthrough

A scheduled tour a week out can be staged. A supplier who agrees on the spot to a 15-minute video call this week, without knowing exactly what you'll ask to see, almost always has nothing to hide — because they own the floor they're showing you.

You Can Check

Propose a short, unannounced walkthrough this week and don't specify in advance what you want to see — a real factory treats this as routine, since it shortens their own sales cycle too.

We schedule in-person visits and confirm the access actually matches what was shown on any earlier video call.

2. The Catalog Spans Too Many Unrelated Categories

A single profile selling electronics, furniture, and auto parts isn't a specialized factory — it's someone reselling from multiple sources.

A buyer sourcing BYD-style alloy wheel rims requested the full product catalog from a supplier listed as a "wheel rim factory" on Alibaba, gold supplier badge included. The PDF that came back ran to 40 pages: rims on page one, then folding camping chairs, phone cases, and a run of USB power banks by page twelve. Nothing on those later pages shared a factory, a mold shop, or even a city with the rims. In Chinese company registration, a business name containing 贸易 (trade) or 进出口 (import-export) marks it as a trading company by legal designation, not a manufacturer — and a catalog like this one usually maps straight onto that structure, sourcing each category from a different subcontractor and never touching the product itself. That registration detail is checkable directly, before any call is scheduled: the buyer pulled the supplier's business license number off their Alibaba profile and ran it through a Chinese company registry lookup, which listed the registered scope as general trade, not automotive parts manufacturing.

You Can Check

Count how many unrelated categories appear in their Alibaba catalog.

What We Verify On-Site

We confirm on the floor what percentage of the catalog is actually manufactured there versus resold.

3. They Can't Show Their Own Production Capacity

A real factory knows, without hesitating, how many lines and workers it has dedicated to your category.

On a call about a brake caliper and suspension arm order, one buyer asked how many CNC machining centers and QC staff were dedicated to automotive components specifically — not the total headcount printed on the sales rep's business card. The first answer was "more than 50 workers." Asked again for lines instead of headcount, the number became "several," then, a few messages later, "it depends on the order." A factory running its own machining floor for calipers and control arms can give a line count without hesitating, because it schedules those lines every morning. A supplier subcontracting the machining out gives a headcount instead, because headcount is the only number that's actually theirs. The buyer's follow-up question — which specific line was running the current order that week — got no answer at all, just a repeated offer to "check with the factory and get back to you," which is an odd sentence for someone claiming to be standing in that factory.

You Can Check

Ask for the number of production lines and workers assigned to your specific product.

What We Verify On-Site

We count lines and workers in person and compare them against the stated capacity.

4. Delivery Times Are Unusually Short for the Volume

If your order volume should take real production weeks but they offer near-immediate shipping, it's likely already sitting as someone else's inventory.

You Can Check

Compare the offered lead time against the industry standard for that volume and product category.

What We Verify On-Site

We check whether the order is actually in production or already exists as another supplier's stock.

5. The Per-Unit Price Barely Changes Between Small and Large Orders

Real factory pricing has a shape: the steepest drop happens right at the MOQ threshold, where setup and tooling costs get absorbed — each volume tier after that gives a progressively smaller discount, not a flat, evenly-spaced line. A supplier who can't explain the step, or just multiplies a flat percentage down for every quantity, usually isn't pricing off their own production costs.

A buyer sourcing LED tail light assemblies got a quote of $8.40 per unit at 500 pieces and $8.10 per unit at 5,000 pieces — a 4% difference across a tenfold jump in volume. Asked to explain the curve, the supplier repeated that it was "our best price" without addressing tooling, injection-mold amortization, or the LED driver board's own volume pricing from its component supplier. A real assembly line has fixed costs — the injection mold for the housing, the jig that positions the driver board — that get absorbed early and then largely stop moving the per-unit number after a few thousand units. What should flatten out near the top of that curve instead sat nearly flat across the whole range, which is what happens when a trading company applies a fixed percentage margin over whatever its own subcontractor already quoted at every volume tier.

You Can Check

Request quotes at 3 different volumes and see if they can explain why the price changes the way it does — not just that it does.

What We Verify On-Site

We compare the quoted pricing structure against known raw material and labor cost benchmarks for the category.

6. They Can't Explain the Production Process in Technical Detail

Product questions can be answered by any salesperson. Process questions can only be answered by someone who actually runs the line.

One buyer, mid-negotiation on a batch of aftermarket wheel rims, asked what tolerance the CNC lathe held on the hub bore diameter, and whether that tolerance changed for a two-piece forged rim versus a one-piece cast one. The reply: "our quality is very good, tolerance is very tight, no problem." Pressed a second time for an actual number, the answer moved to "we can discuss during production." A machinist running that lathe every day has the tolerance memorized in microns, because he sets the fixture to it every shift — the same reason a real supplier answering a tooling question gives a feature-specific number instead of a comfort phrase like "tighter than normal" or "no problem." When nobody who actually touched the machine is on the call, the answer stays vague no matter how many times the question gets asked.

You Can Check

Ask a specific question about the manufacturing process itself, not the product, and judge whether the answer is specific or generic.

What We Verify On-Site

We ask the same technical questions in Mandarin, on the floor, where evasive answers are far easier to catch.

What to Do With These Signs

No single sign here is definitive proof — some trading companies are transparent, reliable partners who tell you exactly what they are. The real risk is not knowing, and paying factory price for a relationship that's actually costing you an extra 10-30% you didn't budget for. Two or more of these signs together is reason enough to confirm who you're actually dealing with before committing to the order.
