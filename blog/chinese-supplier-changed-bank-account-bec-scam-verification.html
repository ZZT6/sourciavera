Chinese Supplier Changed Bank Account? How to Verify Before Wiring

Payment Security
SourciaVera TeamNov 20, 2026 · Updated Nov 23, 2026 · 10 min read

The short answer: Stop the wire immediately whenever a Chinese supplier emails new bank details mid-order. Confirm the change on a live video call, verify the 18-digit Unified Social Credit Code on China's GSXT registry, and check the beneficiary name before sending any deposit or balance.

Verified as of November 23, 2026.

Why Bank Account Changes Are the Top BEC Wire Trap

Business Email Compromise (BEC) is one of the costliest payment threats in cross-border trade. According to the FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) 2025 Annual Report, BEC generated 24,768 complaints and approximately $3.04 billion to $3.05 billion in reported losses—up from 21,442 complaints and $2.77 billion in 2024.

In China sourcing, attackers rarely break bank encryption. Instead, they compromise a supplier's mailbox or register a look-alike domain—swapping one letter such as l for i—and wait quietly until production finishes and your 70% balance is due. Then a familiar thread suddenly sends an urgent PDF saying the factory's usual USD account is "under audit" and asks you to pay a new account instead.

Two Real Corporate Cases That Prove Nobody Is Too Big to Fall for a Bank-Change Email

Many importers assume spoofed bank-change emails only catch first-time buyers. Federal court records and SEC filings prove otherwise: even Silicon Valley giants with full finance departments wired tens of millions because nobody picked up the phone outside the email thread.

1. The $121 Million Google & Facebook Heist: A Look-Alike Asian Hardware Vendor

Between 2013 and 2015, a Lithuanian national named Evaldas Rimasauskas used a simple public fact: both Google and Facebook bought millions of dollars of hardware from Taiwanese manufacturer Quanta Computer. Instead of hacking Google's servers, he incorporated a company in Latvia using the exact same name (Quanta Computer), opened bank accounts in Latvia and Cyprus, and emailed forged invoices and contracts telling accounts payable to wire funds to the "updated" beneficiary accounts.

According to the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ), Google wired roughly $23 million and Facebook wired roughly $99 million—over $121 million total—before the scheme was uncovered. In December 2019, Rimasauskas was sentenced to 5 years in federal prison and ordered to forfeit nearly $49.7 million. If Google and Facebook wired $121 million to a spoofed vendor account, imagine how easily an $18,000 container balance can be diverted.

2. Ubiquiti Networks: $46.7 Million Wired Out of a Hong Kong Subsidiary

The second landmark case hits directly at China–Hong Kong trade flows. In August 2015, U.S. networking company Ubiquiti Networks disclosed in a U.S. SEC Form 8-K filing that social engineering and spoofed emails targeting its finance team caused $46.7 million in unauthorized wire transfers out of a Hong Kong subsidiary to third-party overseas accounts. The subsequent review flagged material weaknesses in verification controls for overseas wire requests.

The lesson for any China importer is direct: when an email suddenly claims "our mainland account is under tax audit, please wire the 70% balance to this new Hong Kong account," you are looking at the exact playbook behind the largest BEC wire losses in corporate history.

How the Takeover Actually Starts: A Real "Mailbox 100% Full" Phishing Attack Sent to Our Own Inbox

Buyers often ask: "How do BEC scammers actually break into a real Chinese factory's email thread to read our invoices?" Rarely via cinematic hacking. Almost always, it begins with a routine credential-phishing trap polished enough to fool busy sales reps and office staff.

We received this exact attack firsthand at our own corporate inbox (info@sourciavera.com). The email displayed a clean yellow banner reading "SYSTEM NOTICE: AUTOMATED ALERT" and a red headline—"Your mailbox is 100% full"—warning that our account could no longer send or receive messages unless we clicked the red "Manage Storage Space" button. Clicking through led straight to a fake login prompt demanding our email username and password.

How our founder spotted the trap in seconds (5 checks you can apply immediately)

  1. The 100%-full contradiction: If a corporate mailbox were genuinely at 100% capacity, incoming messages would bounce—you would not receive a glossy HTML alert with a red button inviting you to log in on an external page.
  2. Look-alike sender & off-domain button URL: Attackers spoof your domain display name or register a look-alike domain; hovering over "Manage Storage Space" (without clicking) reveals a destination URL unrelated to your real mail host (such as Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, or Zoho).
  3. Real mail hosts never harvest passwords via email buttons: To check mailbox storage, navigate directly to your email admin portal in a fresh browser tab—never through an embedded alert button.
  4. Manufactured operational panic: Threatening that you cannot send or receive business emails pushes rushed factory sales reps or importers to type in their password in 10 seconds without checking the browser address bar.
  5. The direct link to supplier bank-change fraud: The moment one employee at your Chinese supplier falls for an identical email, the attacker logs into the real mailbox silently, sets a hidden forwarding rule, monitors your order for weeks, and hijacks the thread right when your 70% final balance is due.

5-Step Protocol to Verify a Supplier Bank Account Change

Before releasing a single dollar—especially before paying the final balance after a pre-shipment inspection—run these five checks in order:

  1. Verify the entity on China's official registry: Look up the supplier's 18-digit Unified Social Credit Code on the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (GSXT). Confirm legal status, registered address, and exact Chinese company name.
  2. Match the beneficiary name letter-for-letter: Under standard corporate banking rules, a Chinese mainland factory's official USD account carries the English translation of its registered Chinese entity name—not an unrelated trading house, individual person, or third-country company.
  3. Verify out-of-band by phone or WhatsApp: Never reply to the email announcing the change. Call the factory manager using a verified phone number from your original contract or earlier invoice, not the phone signature inside the new email.
  4. Require a live video check on the factory floor: Use a live video factory verification call where your contact stands beside active machinery, holds up the stamped business license, and reads the updated beneficiary name aloud on camera.
  5. Tie payment release to physical cargo verification: When paying a balance on machinery or industrial equipment from China, pair bank verification with a statistical check using ISO 2859-1 AQL inspection standards—understanding Acceptable Quality Limit (AQL), the maximum number of defective units allowed in a random inspection sample—before funds leave your bank.

What to Do Immediately If You Already Wired Funds (Critical 24–72 Hour Window)

If funds have already left your account to a suspect beneficiary, act by time window and payment rail without losing a minute:

  • Within 24–48 hours — SWIFT Recall with MT103: Call your sending bank's international wire fraud desk within the first hour with your SWIFT MT103 reference and request an urgent fraud recall message to both the intermediary and receiving banks.
  • Within 72 hours — File an FBI IC3 Report (Financial Fraud Kill Chain): Because most cross-border USD wires clear through U.S. correspondent banks, the FBI IC3 Recovery Asset Team specifically prioritizes freezing fraudulent wires reported inside the first 72 hours.
  • Card (<$5,000) vs. Wire (≥$10,000) escalation: Credit card payments typically carry a 60–120 day chargeback window; for T/T wires over $10,000 where funds have already been withdrawn, preserve raw .eml headers, the stamped PI, MT103, and 18-digit USCC for a licensed commercial or criminal attorney.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do legitimate Chinese suppliers rarely switch beneficiary accounts mid-order?

Chinese export factories tie their USD receiving accounts to customs registration, tax filings, and commercial contracts. An unannounced email switch to an unrelated Hong Kong trading name, personal account, or third-country bank is a primary Business Email Compromise warning sign rather than standard factory banking practice.

How can I verify a Chinese supplier bank change before sending a wire?

Freeze payment immediately, confirm the change on a live video call with the factory representative holding their business license, verify the 18-digit Unified Social Credit Code on GSXT, match the beneficiary name letter-for-letter, and confirm via an independent phone number from your original contract.

Should I ever wire a factory balance to a personal or unrelated Hong Kong account?

Never wire funds to a personal account or an unrelated entity without documented corporate proof. If a real factory uses an affiliated Hong Kong subsidiary, require official shareholding documentation, a company-chopped authorization letter, and live video confirmation with the legal representative before paying.

What should I do if I already wired money to a spoofed bank account?

Contact your sending bank immediately to request a SWIFT wire recall and alert the receiving bank's fraud desk. File an FBI IC3 report right away—the IC3 Recovery Asset Team focuses on freezing fraudulent transfers within the critical first 72 hours.
