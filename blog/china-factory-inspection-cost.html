Why Is Inspection the Cheapest Line in Your Budget?

Three reasons:

  • Cost is both why buyers skip it and why they regret it. The cost that hurts is that of a bad shipment that has already left China.
  • The problems that hurt most are the ones nobody looked for. They surface after the goods are on the water.
  • Before shipment you can still say "fix it first." Afterwards you are negotiating from a weak position.

Which Inspection Do You Need: PSI, DPI, IPC or a Factory Audit?

Buyers often ask me for "an inspection" when they actually need something else. Choosing the right type is half the savings:

  • Pre-Shipment Inspection (PSI): Done when 80–100% of goods are finished. The inspector checks quantity, quality, measurements, labels, and packing against your spec sheet. This is the most common type and the minimum I recommend for any order over $3,000.
  • During Production Inspection (DPI): Done when 20–40% of production is complete. It catches issues early, when they can still be fixed without scrapping the entire batch. Best for orders where quality is critical and the factory is new to you.
  • Initial Production Check (IPC): Done when raw materials arrive and first samples come off the line. Used for high-complexity or high-value orders to make sure materials meet spec before production begins.
  • Factory Audit: Assesses the factory's overall capability, processes, certifications, and management system. It's not product-specific — it tells you whether a factory can reliably produce to your standards before you place any order.

What a factory check looks like in practice: in the case of the BYD Seagull spare wheel, the factory's first spec sheet had the wrong offset, confused with the center bore. Our team required a stamped re-check and the factory corrected the figure to the real offset, ET 30. The takeaway: no listing shows you that.

Full Inspection vs. Sampling Inspection: Which Do You Need?

Most factory inspections in China use statistical sampling. The inspector doesn't check every unit; they pull a defined number of pieces based on your order quantity and apply AQL (Acceptance Quality Limit; glossary) tables to decide pass or fail. For most standard orders, this is sufficient and cost-effective.

A full inspection — also called 100% inspection — means every single unit is examined individually against your specifications, instead of extrapolating from a sample. The man-day rate is the same as sampling inspection; what changes is the number of days required, which depends on how many units need to be checked and how long each unit takes.

In my experience, sampling is usually the right choice when:

  • Your order is mid-to-large volume and your product has a consistent, low-defect history
  • You have an established relationship with the factory
  • Product failure consequences are limited to returns or replacements, not safety risks

Full inspection makes sense when:

  • Every defective unit reaching your customer carries disproportionate cost — in returns, safety liability, or reputational damage
  • You are placing a first order with a new factory and cannot afford quality variance
  • You previously received a defective batch and need certainty before the next shipment
  • Your product has safety-critical specifications (electrical components, structural parts, regulated goods)
  • Your order is small enough that a statistical sample would be too small to be meaningful

Because the man-days for a full inspection scale with both order volume and how complex each unit is to check, the total varies by order. Send me your order details and I'll give you a specific quote.

What Does a Pre-Shipment Inspection Check?

A standard pre-shipment inspection is not a vague walkthrough. Every checkpoint uses defined sample sizes, physical instruments, and pass/fail limits (see our free printable PSI inspection checklist PDF):

  • Quantity & packing readiness: Confirm at least 80% of the purchase order is finished and packed in export cartons (100% for strict buyer terms), verify total carton count against the packing list, and weigh random master cartons against declared gross/net weights (flagging variance beyond ±3%).
  • Visual & workmanship check under ISO 2859-1: Pull a statistical sample at General Inspection Level II — for an illustrative 3,000-unit lot, Code Letter K requires 125 sampled units, with standard limits of Critical 0/1, Major AQL 2.5 (7 accept / 8 reject), and Minor AQL 4.0 (10 accept / 11 reject). Each unit is inspected at roughly 50 cm distance under 500+ lux lighting. You can calculate your exact sample size in our ISO 2859-1 AQL calculator.
  • Dimensional & weight verification: Measure critical dimensions with a digital caliper (0.01 mm resolution), tape measure, and calibrated scale against your approved golden sample and technical drawing, recording actual readings rather than "looks close."
  • On-site function & power-on test: Verify rated voltage/frequency labels (e.g., 110–120V / 60Hz for North America and Mexico), run switch/cycle checks, and confirm basic operation. Boundary note: destructive chemical, salt-spray, high-voltage hi-pot, or regulatory compliance certifications require an accredited CNAS or ISO 17025 testing lab rather than a field PSI.
  • Barcodes, shipping marks & destination labels: Scan UPC/EAN/FNSKU barcodes with a reader to confirm readability, verify master carton shipping marks, and check destination labeling (such as Spanish commercial labels for Mexico).
  • Carton drop test (ISTA 1A procedure): Drop a sealed sample export carton 10 times (1 corner, 3 edges, 6 faces) from the weight-matched height — for example, 61 cm (24 in) for cartons weighing 9–19 kg (20–41 lb) or 76 cm (30 in) for cartons under 9 kg (20 lb) — then open the carton to verify inner dividers and product condition.

Most inspections deliver a written report with photographs and unedited video within 24–48 hours. The pass/fail result tells you immediately whether to authorize balance payment and loading or require rework before the goods leave China — which is the last moment you still have real leverage.

Is Inspection Worth the Cost? Run the Math

Take an illustrative order: 300 units at $40/unit, totaling $12,000 in order value. A one-man-day pre-shipment inspection at $399 equals 3.33% of order value. If the inspection catches a defect that would otherwise force you to scrap or rework $3,000 in goods (25% of the batch), the inspection fee is less than one-seventh of the loss avoided.

When Should You Never Skip Inspection?

  • First order with any new supplier — inspect. It is the point where you know the least about them.
  • Orders over $5,000 — the math makes inspection cost-effective
  • Products with safety implications — electrical goods, children's products, anything with structural requirements
  • Orders going to Amazon FBA — Amazon's rejection and return penalties for non-compliant goods are brutal; a one-time $399 inspection is small next to a container of goods worth tens of thousands being refused at the warehouse or returned by your customers
  • Orders with tight spec requirements — precise dimensions, certified materials, performance benchmarks

How Do You Choose an Independent Inspector?

For standardized, internationally recognized reports, the big three — SGS, Intertek, and Bureau Veritas — are your safest options. QIMA (formerly Asia Inspection) offers faster booking and competitive pricing. For Guangzhou specifically, a local sourcing agent with inspection capability can offer better coverage, video documentation, and faster turnaround than a large global firm routing everything through a central team.

Whoever you choose, confirm the inspector has no affiliation or financial relationship with the supplier. Bluntly: an inspector recommended by your factory is not independent.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a pre-shipment inspection in China cost per man-day?

SourciaVera charges $399 per man-day for a pre-shipment inspection (PSI) and $499 per man-day for a factory audit, both with unedited on-site video and a written report in English or Spanish. Major multinational inspection agencies generally quote by the man-day in China, with extra charges for remote travel or rush scheduling.

How many units can one inspector check in a single man-day?

Under ISO 2859-1 General Inspection Level II, one man-day covers statistical AQL sampling for most orders up to roughly 3,000 to 10,000 standard units (125 to 200 sampled pieces), provided goods are packed and accessible. If you require 100% unit-by-unit inspection, required man-days scale by piece count and test time per unit.

What is the minimum order value where a $399 inspection makes financial sense?

As a math benchmark (illustrative), $399 equals 7.98% of a $5,000 order, 3.33% of a $12,000 order, and 1.33% of a $30,000 order. For first orders over $5,000 with a new Chinese supplier, a single defective batch typically costs far more in rework, freight, or lost inventory than one man-day of inspection.

Does an on-site pre-shipment inspection replace laboratory safety certification?

No. An on-site PSI verifies quantity, workmanship under ISO 2859-1 AQL sampling, physical dimensions, basic power-on function, labels, and ISTA 1A carton drop resistance. Destructive chemical, salt-spray, high-voltage, or regulatory compliance certifications require an accredited CNAS or ISO 17025 testing laboratory.
