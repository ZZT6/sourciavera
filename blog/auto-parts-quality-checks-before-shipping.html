6 Quality Checks Every Auto Parts Buyer Should Run Before Shipping

Published Aug 4, 2026 · Updated Oct 22, 2026 · 7 min read · Zhang Peng (Miguel Lopez), Founder
Back to Blog

Most buyers treat quality control as something that happens at the end, a box to tick once production is finished. I think that's backwards, especially for auto parts. By the time a container is loaded, the decisions that decide whether it's good were made weeks earlier: which mold the factory used, which alloy went into the furnace, which chipset ended up on the board.

The short answer: before an auto parts shipment leaves China, check six things: dimensional fitment against the OEM spec, the material or alloy certificate, coating durability, tested LED output and IP rating, packaging for the real route, and a pre-shipment AQL (the agreed defect limit for an inspection sample; glossary) sample. Each one targets a failure that shows up over and over in this category, and each one is far cheaper to catch at the factory than after the container lands.

Our team visits factories in person, and the founder still joins many of the visits. Auto parts go wrong in specific, repeatable ways: wrong fitment, overstated wattage, coatings that don't survive the trip. These are the 6 checks we rely on to catch those failures before the container leaves port.

Why Check Auto Parts Before the Container Is Sealed?

Three reasons:

  • The decisions are made weeks earlier. Which mold, which alloy and which chipset went in were decided long before the container was loaded.
  • The failures are specific and repeatable. Wrong fitment, overstated wattage and coatings that do not survive the trip show up over and over in this category.
  • It is far cheaper at the factory. A defect found before sealing means a held shipment; found later, it becomes a return, a customs problem or a warranty claim nobody budgeted for.

1. Will the Part Actually Fit? Dimensional Check Against the OEM Spec

"Fits [car model]" is a marketing claim, not a spec. Bolt pattern, offset and center bore have to match numbers, not a listing description.

One pitfall is ordering by application, the way you would at a parts counter: “16-inch rim for a Nissan Versa”, “brake pads for a Chevrolet Aveo”. A factory in China does not know the part the way a counter salesperson does; it quotes whatever it has in the mould that looks closest. Always send measurements and the OEM part number, not just make, model and year.

A real order. A Latin American client needed a spare wheel kit for the BYD Seagull. The factory's first spec sheet listed the offset as ET 54.1, PCD 4×100 and a 54.1 mm hub bore — but 54.1 mm is the hub bore, not the offset, and the real offset turned out to be ET 30 once we required a stamped re-check. Several factories we evaluated confirmed their offset verbally, with no way to prove it before shipment. The factory we chose was the one willing to re-check its own numbers in writing and send a spec sheet with real manufacturing data, not a catalog page. The takeaway: proof beats promises — even from the factory itself. The whole order is written up in the BYD Seagull spare wheel offset case.

The typical industry failure. A buyer orders a set of 18-inch rims listed as a direct fit for a popular crossover. The factory works from its own reference mold instead of the OEM spec sheet, and the wheels arrive with the bolt pattern right but the offset off by 6mm. Six millimeters doesn't sound like much until the inner lip rubs the strut on full lock, or the outer face fouls the fender liner. Owner forums are full of the same complaint under different part numbers: wheels that mount but don't actually fit, valve stem holes in the wrong place, a rim that's fine for a generic 5-lug pattern but wrong for the specific hub.

On a container of 400 wheels, you won't catch that by looking at one sample under showroom lighting. It takes a caliper measurement against the real OEM number, on units pulled from the pallet that's about to ship, not the one the factory keeps for visitors. Get the offset wrong at scale and the result isn't a bad review. It's a full container on a return shipment, with the freight paid twice.

Request exact measurements against the OEM spec sheet, not just a fitment claim.

2. Is It the Alloy You Paid For? Material Grade Certification

Load-bearing parts live or die by the alloy grade actually used, not the grade quoted in the listing. The two are not always the same.

For aluminium wheels, what is usually quoted is A356 alloy with T6 heat treatment. On bolts and fasteners the grade (8.8, 10.9) is stamped on the head. A trading company may not even have access to the certificate of the batch you will receive; it may only forward what it was given. That is why it pays to confirm first whether your supplier is a real factory or a trading company (see factory or trading company).

You Can Check

Ask for the alloy grade or material certificate for any load-bearing part.

3. Will the Coating Survive a Winter? Finish Durability

A finish that looks fine on day one can fail within months if nobody tested it for the conditions it will really face.

Powder-coated brake calipers are a good example because the failure is so well documented across the industry. They look showroom-fresh out of the box, survive a season, and then start bubbling and rusting at the edges where the coating didn't fully cure or wasn't thick enough over the rough spots of the casting.

An illustrative example (the numbers are not from a client): the spec sheet says the coating passed salt spray testing, and the attached report says 96 hours. If an independent lab runs a sample pulled from the production batch instead of a hand-picked showpiece and red rust shows up on the mounting ears at 48 hours, that is less than half the claimed rating. A coating like that will look perfect in the listing photos and start failing within a year of your customers driving through winter road salt. The takeaway: the gap is between "salt spray tested" and "tested to what result, on which sample". You only see it if someone checks the actual certificate against the batch that's shipping, not a reference unit kept in a drawer.

You Can Check

Ask if salt spray testing has been done, and for how many hours.

What Our Team Verifies On-Site

We can commission an independent salt spray test report if none exists yet.

4. Is the LED as Bright as Claimed? Lumen Output and IP Rating

Chipset datasheets show theoretical maximums. The output of the finished, housed unit is a different number.

LED headlight and taillight assemblies are where the gap between claimed and tested output tends to be widest. A listing claims 12,000 lumens per side. That figure comes from the chipset maker's datasheet under lab bench conditions, not from the finished unit. Measure the real assembly properly, with a power meter for the watts it actually draws and an integrating sphere for the light it actually puts out (a lux meter reads illuminance at one spot and can't give you either number), and the result usually lands well below the claim once housing losses, driver limits and real 12V draw are counted.

For a headlight that's supposed to meet a minimum road-legal output, this isn't marketing fluff a buyer can shrug off. It can fail a vehicle inspection in the destination market, or become a liability question after a nighttime collision. The IP rating usually comes with the same inflation: "IP67" on the listing and no test report behind it, which matters the first time a customer takes the car through a car wash. Testing the finished unit against the claimed number is the only way to know before 2,000 units are already on the water.

You Can Check

Ask for the tested lumen output and IP rating of the finished product, not the chipset's rated maximum.

What Our Team Verifies On-Site

We read the finished unit's power draw with a power meter and can arrange an independent integrating-sphere test for luminous flux, then compare both with the claimed spec before you commit to the order.

5. Will It Arrive in One Piece? Packaging Drop-Test

A product can pass inspection perfectly and still arrive broken if nobody tested the packaging for the actual shipping route. Cargo insurance may not make that good: an insurer's guide lists insufficient packing among the exclusions (see cargo insurance on a shipment from China).

For Mexico, the route does not end at the port: the container arrives at Manzanillo or Lázaro Cárdenas and continues by trailer to the Bajío, Mexico City or Monterrey, with several handling stages on the way. The parts that suffer most are those with fragile or protruding features: headlight and tail light lenses, mounting ears, mirrors and bumper covers.

You Can Check

Ask what packaging materials are used and whether they've been drop-tested for your route.

6. How Do You Catch Defects Before the Container Is Sealed? Pre-Shipment AQL Sampling

This is the one check that catches most defects before they become your problem, but only if someone actually runs it before the container is sealed.

The problem. Suspension bushings and control arm assemblies are small and cheap per unit, so buyers are tempted to skip formal sampling and trust the factory's own QC sign-off. Bushings molded off-center, or noticeably softer than the specified durometer, don't show up in a five-second visual check. Both show up a few thousand miles later as premature wear, clunking, or a control arm that won't hold alignment.

How the sampling plan works. For a lot of 3,000 polyurethane control arm bushings, AQL 2.5 at general inspection level II calls for a sample of 125 units, with an accept number of 7 and a reject number of 8 major defects.

An illustrative result. Say the inspector finds 10 major defects in that sample of 125, against a reject number of 8. That is a clear fail, with no room for argument, and it means a shipment held before sealing instead of a warranty claim nobody budgeted for six months later. (The numbers are an example, not a client result.)

The takeaway. Agreeing the AQL level in writing is what turns "this batch looks off" into a decision the factory can't argue with.

You Can Check

Agree on an AQL level (commonly 2.5 for general merchandise) in writing before production starts.

Summary: Six Checks Before the Container Is Sealed

Wheels, lighting and structural components carry safety and liability consequences that a t-shirt or a phone case never will. A fitment error or an untested coating isn't just a bad review. It's a return shipment, a compliance problem at customs, or worse.

The biggest lesson for me has been that a factory's willingness to prove a spec tells you more than the spec itself. The suppliers who send the installation video, the real test report or the mill certificate without being chased are usually the ones you can build recurring orders with. The ones who answer "yes, it fits" and nothing more are the ones I slow down for. For this category, these 6 checks aren't extra caution. They're the minimum.

If you are starting out, the order I would follow is: confirm who makes the part (see what a factory audit before importing to Mexico covers), fix the specifications in writing, and inspect last.

In short, run the six checks before the container leaves: fitment, material, coating, LED output, packaging and an AQL sample. If you skip them, a fitment error or an untested coating can come back as a return shipment or a compliance problem, with your cash tied up and your next order delayed.
